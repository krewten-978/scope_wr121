# U1L11LL – Logic Lab Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L11LL/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L11LL**).

**Paired reading:** Week 11 — analogy, source case, target case, relevant similarity, disanalogy, overextension, decorative comparison, analogy test.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Analogy:** reasoning from a likeness between two cases.
- **Source case:** the familiar or comparison case used to illuminate the argument.
- **Target case:** the case, person, decision, or policy the speaker wants judged.
- **Relevant similarity:** the likeness that matters for the conclusion being argued.
- **Disanalogy:** an important difference between source and target that limits the comparison.
- **Overextension:** forcing an analogy to prove a broader claim than its relevant similarity supports.
- **Decorative comparison:** a vivid comparison that adds style or emphasis but does little proof-work.
- **Irrelevant proof / missing the point (review):** evidence answers a different or narrower question than the conclusion requires.
- **Only-translation (review):** “Only F do X” standardly means all that do X are F (not the reverse “all F do X”).

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Lunch schedule and traffic pattern (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Source case:** changing the traffic pattern outside school (or needing clear advance signs for a traffic change). **Target case:** changing the lunch schedule. **Conclusion:** the school should publish the new lunch schedule before the change begins.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “traffic change / road signs” for source; “new lunch times / lunch schedule change” for target; “publish the schedule in advance,” “tell students before the change,” or “announce lunch times ahead of time” for the conclusion. Students may add that the relevant similarity is advance notice preventing chaos; that addition is helpful but not required by this prompt.
- **Required meaning:** All three components the prompt requests must appear: source, target, and conclusion. Swapping source and target is a material error.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly identifies source, target, and conclusion
- 1 pt: Accurately identifies two of the three, or all three with one materially vague
- 0 pts: Swaps source and target without recovery, invents a different conclusion, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the labels “source” and “target” if the two cases are clearly distinguished in the right roles.
- Do not require a relevant-similarity analysis on this item; Part 2 covers that skill.
- Ordinary paraphrases are enough; exact worksheet wording is not required.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Research paper and building (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Source case:** a building (needing a foundation). **Target case:** a research paper / draft (with sources as support). **Conclusion:** the draft needs stronger sources before the introduction is revised.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “building/foundation/construction” for source; “research paper,” “essay draft,” or “my paper’s sources” for target; “fix sources first,” “strengthen the foundation of sources before revising the intro,” or another clear paraphrase that the draft needs stronger sources before introduction work.
- **Required meaning:** Source, target, and conclusion must all be present with correct roles. Do not treat “sources” alone as the source case if the comparison case is the building.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly identifies source, target, and conclusion
- 1 pt: Accurately identifies two of the three, or all three with one materially vague
- 0 pts: Swaps cases, misses the conclusion, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the student to evaluate whether the analogy is strong.
- Accept “paper needs a foundation of sources before upper revisions” as covering target + conclusion if both roles are clear.
- Do not require the word “foundation” if the building comparison and sources-first conclusion are clear.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Captain and point guard (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Relevant similarity:** both roles require noticing the whole team/field, communication, and decisions not reducible to personal statistics. **Important disanalogy:** a point guard is a specific in-game playing position, while team captain is a broader leadership role that may involve conduct, practice habits, representation, or off-court responsibilities.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** For similarity, accept “whole-field awareness,” “communication,” “team vision over stats,” or “leadership beyond scoring.” For disanalogy, accept any real limiting difference: different job scope, captain not necessarily a position, school/team culture duties, coaches choose captains differently, etc.
- **Required meaning:** Both a relevant similarity that supports considering communication and one important limiting difference must appear. A difference that does not limit the captain-selection point is weaker.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States a relevant similarity and a real limiting disanalogy
- 1 pt: Gives only similarity or only disanalogy, or gives both with one off-target
- 0 pts: Treats the cases as identical, gives no usable comparison analysis, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all model similarity details; one accurate relevant likeness is enough.
- Do not require formal labels if both contents are clear.
- A disanalogy may still leave the analogy partly useful; students need not destroy the comparison entirely.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Dress code and job uniform (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Similarity used:** both involve clothing expectations tied to public conduct, appearance standards, or professionalism. **Disanalogy that limits the conclusion:** students are not employees; school discipline is formative; violation seriousness differs; a workplace uniform rule does not automatically justify detention for every school dress-code violation. The analogy may support some standards without proving automatic detention for any violation.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “both set clothing rules,” “public image,” or “professional appearance” for similarity. For limiting disanalogy, accept student vs employee status, different authority/consent, educational purpose, age, severity of response, or “not every violation deserves detention.”
- **Required meaning:** Similarity plus a disanalogy that specifically limits the automatic-detention conclusion. A bare “they are different” without how it limits the penalty is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States the similarity and a disanalogy that limits the detention/automatic-penalty conclusion
- 1 pt: Gives only one side, or a disanalogy that does not clearly limit the conclusion
- 0 pts: Treats the job comparison as automatically decisive for detention, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require opposition to all dress codes; the task is limiting the overstrong penalty conclusion.
- Do not require multiple disanalogies; one well-aimed limit is enough.
- Ordinary language is sufficient.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Phones and candy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The analogy **supports a narrower/practical claim** more than it fully proves every possible phone policy or merely decorates. Open temptations can distract a group even if some individuals resist, so a phone plan during quizzes is reasonable. It does not by itself prove the harshest possible ban, confiscation scheme, or every detail of enforcement.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “supports a narrower claim,” “supports having some phone plan,” or “good limited support for quiz rules.” A careful “proves the whole claim” answer may earn full credit only if the student defines the whole claim modestly as “some plan is needed” and explains why the likeness reaches that far. “Merely decorates” is weak unless the student denies any distraction likeness; that reading is usually too harsh for this example.
- **Required meaning:** The response must choose among whole claim / narrower claim / decoration (or clear equivalent) and explain using relevant similarity or limits. A label alone without explanation is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Judges the analogy as supporting a narrow/practical claim (or a carefully scoped whole claim) and explains with relevant likeness or limit
- 1 pt: Chooses a category with only thin explanation, or explains distraction without a clear whole/narrow/decorate judgment
- 0 pts: Label only, treats the analogy as empty decoration with no reason, accepts unlimited phone policy from the candy image alone, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact words “overextension” or “decorative comparison.”
- Do not require students to invent a full school policy.
- Prefer the narrower-claim reading as canonical; credit other categories only when the explanation preserves the logical force of the example.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – College and winter coat (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The analogy **overextends / tries to prove the whole claim and fails**. Choosing a college and choosing a coat may both involve price and “fit,” so the comparison may support considering cost. It does not prove the cheapest option is always smartest, because college choice also involves program quality, financial aid, debt, distance, academic fit, graduation outcomes, and long-term consequences.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “supports only a narrower claim (consider price),” “proves too much,” “overextends,” or “does not prove cheapest is always smartest.” “Merely decorates” is acceptable only if the student explains that price-is-everything is so weak the coat image adds little real proof; better answers usually treat it as a limited cost comparison overextended into always-cheapest.
- **Required meaning:** The response must reject the always-cheapest conclusion as fully proved and explain via disanalogy or overextension. Accepting the whole claim as proved is incorrect.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Rejects full proof of always-cheapest and explains with relevant disanalogy or overextension (may allow a narrower cost-consideration claim)
- 1 pt: Says price is not everything or “bad analogy” with only weak explanation
- 0 pts: Accepts cheapest-always as proved by the coat comparison, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a fixed list of college factors; one or two real disanalogies are enough.
- Do not require the word “overextension” if the too-strong conclusion is clearly identified.
- Whole/narrow/decorate labels may vary if the explanation correctly limits the claim.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Tutoring garden paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Conclusion:** start the tutoring program with ninth graders only, measure results, then expand. **Source case:** planting one garden/field section first. **Target case:** launching the tutoring program (beginning with ninth grade). **Relevant similarity:** a small pilot can teach what works before scaling. **Important disanalogy:** students are not plants; older students may have different or urgent needs; programs involve fairness, staffing, and varied learners. **Best judgment:** the analogy gives real but limited support for a pilot, especially with the added ninth-grade need point; it does not by itself settle every fairness or priority concern.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Faithful paraphrases of each element. Judgment may lean more supportive of the pilot or more cautious about fairness if the student still treats the analogy as limited rather than empty or decisive for every issue. The objector’s “people are not plants” line may supply the disanalogy.
- **Required meaning:** The prompt’s analogy-test package must be covered: conclusion, source and target, relevant similarity, important disanalogy, and best judgment. Thin paraphrase of the story without judgment is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Includes conclusion, source/target, similarity, disanalogy, and a balanced/limited-support judgment
- 1 pt: Includes most elements but misses judgment or one major mapping piece, or judgment is present with several mapping gaps
- 0 pts: Story paraphrase only, treats the garden image as decisive for every concern or as pure decoration with no analysis, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require Lena’s exact wording or every supporting detail (soil, water, spacing).
- Do not require both “measure for a quarter” and “then expand” if the pilot-then-expand conclusion is clear.
- Source/target may be labeled in ordinary language.

## Question 8: Part 5 Q1 – Review: mural budget (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Earlier-week defect: **irrelevant proof / missing the point**. The committee may have proved that the mural would make the hallway brighter and more interesting, but not that the mural is the best use of the entire arts budget compared with alternatives.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “missing the point,” “irrelevant proof,” “nearby claim,” “proves improvement not best use of the whole budget,” or “wrong question / doesn’t compare alternatives.”
- **Required meaning:** Name a prior-week defect type (or clear ordinary equivalent) and briefly explain the mismatch between hallway-improvement proof and best-use-of-entire-budget conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Names missing-the-point / irrelevant proof (or clear equivalent) and explains the proof-to-claim mismatch
- 0 pts: Calls it only circular or only false without the mismatch, accepts the leap as proved, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact Week 10 vocabulary if the mismatch is clearly explained.
- Do not require a full alternative budget plan.

## Question 9: Part 5 Q2 – Review: reliable sources (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Under the standard reading of **only**, “Only reliable sources correct their errors publicly” means that all sources that correct errors publicly are reliable (not “all reliable sources correct errors publicly”). Structure: All public-error-correctors are reliable; this website corrected an error publicly; therefore this website is reliable. **The conclusion follows** (valid categorical form on that reading).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept plain-language exclusivity (“if it corrects publicly, it counts as reliable on that standard”), or mood/form labels that preserve direction. Accept “valid” / “follows” when paired with a correct only-handling.
- **Required meaning:** Identify the structure problem-or-form being used and judge whether the conclusion follows. Full credit requires directionally correct **only** handling and a matching follows judgment.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Structure consistent with standard only-translation and correctly says the conclusion follows; or clear exclusive reading in ordinary language plus follows
- 0 pts: Reverses **only** into “all reliable sources correct errors publicly” and calls the argument invalid on that mistranslation; says valid/invalid with no usable structure; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a medieval mood name.
- **Content note:** An earlier key version reversed **only** and treated the item as an invalid converse. Grade the printed wording. Students who reverse **only** and diagnose invalidity are not correctly analyzing this prompt.
- If a student flags two English readings and shows which yields follows vs. not, credit the accurate exclusive reading as the standard logical answer.
