# U1L8LL – Logic Lab Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 12

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L8LL/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L8LL**).

**Paired reading:** Week 8 — equivocation, ambiguous middle, fixed-meaning rewrite.

**Example sources (key only):** Standard classroom dual-sense patterns modernized — *bat* (animal vs sports equipment), *fair* (just vs festival), *plane* (aircraft vs geometry), *present* (in attendance vs gift), *sound* (logically solid vs working audio). Do not require students to name sources. Do not reuse Week 2 worksheet lines (*right*, *free*, feathers/light, star, end, nobody).

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Equivocation:** one word used in two senses inside one argument.
- **Ambiguous middle:** the apparent middle term changes meaning, so it is not one class or idea connecting the premises.
- **Sense A / Sense B:** the two contextually relevant meanings of the shifting term; students need not use these labels unless a prompt asks for them.
- **Fixed-meaning rewrite:** restating the relevant occurrences so each meaning is explicit and the hidden shift can no longer pass as one shared term.
- **Form test:** if the premises were true under one stable reading, would the conclusion have to follow?
- **Fact test:** are the premises true, false, doubtful, or unproved?
- **Fallacy autopsy:** the conclusion, apparent proof, and defect requested in the Week 7 review question.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Bats (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The shifting term is **bat/bats**. In the first premise it means a flying mammal or animal; in the second it means a baseball bat or other piece of sports equipment ordered by the coach.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “cave-dwelling animal” versus “baseball/cricket/sports bat,” or comparable ordinary descriptions that distinguish the living animal from the object used in a sport.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the repeated term and state both contextually relevant meanings. The prompt does not require an explanation of the conclusion once those three items are supplied.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies bat/bats and clearly states both the animal meaning and sports-equipment meaning
- 1 pt: Identifies the term but gives only one clear meaning, or recognizes two meanings without identifying the shifting word
- 0 pts: Gives two meanings that do not fit the printed sentences, treats the coach as ordering animals, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “equivocation,” “ambiguous middle,” “flying mammal,” or “baseball equipment.”
- Singular/plural differences do not matter. “Bat means an animal in one sentence and something used to hit a ball in the other” earns full credit.
- Do not require a separate statement that the conclusion fails; the student-facing prompt asks only for the term and both meanings.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Fair (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The shifting term is **fair/fairs**. In the first premise it means an outdoor festival or public event with vendors; in the second it means just, equitable, impartial, or correct as a referee's call.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “festival/carnival/event” versus “just/unbiased/reasonable/correct,” provided each meaning is tied to the correct sentence.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify fair as the repeated word and distinguish the event meaning from the justice or correctness meaning.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies fair/fairs and clearly states both the festival/event meaning and just/equitable/correct meaning
- 1 pt: Identifies the term but gives only one clear meaning, or gives both meanings without clearly identifying the shifting term
- 0 pts: Gives meanings that do not fit the two printed uses, treats the referee's call as a festival, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact pair “outdoor festival” and “equitable.”
- Accept ordinary language such as “a fair you attend” versus “a fair decision.”
- Do not withhold credit because the first sentence uses the plural *fairs* and the second uses *fair*.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Planes (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The apparent middle term **plane/planes** changes from aircraft in the first premise to flat geometric surfaces or figures in the worksheet in the second. Because these are different classes, there is no single middle idea connecting things that fly under their own power to the worksheet's geometric planes.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “airplanes” versus “flat surfaces in geometry,” “flying vehicles” versus “mathematical planes,” or an explanation that the same spelling hides two unrelated kinds of thing and therefore cannot carry the inference.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must explain both meanings and why the meaning change breaks the connection between the premises. Merely saying “planes is ambiguous” is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes aircraft from geometric planes and explains that the two meanings cannot function as one connecting middle term
- 1 pt: Identifies plane/planes or both meanings but does not clearly explain the failed connection
- 0 pts: Treats geometric planes as aircraft, gives an unrelated objection, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal labels “middle term,” “M class,” “ambiguous middle,” or “equivocation.”
- Accept a concise explanation such as “the first planes are airplanes, but the worksheet planes are mathematical surfaces, so the premise about flying does not apply to them.”
- Do not require a rewrite; this prompt asks only why the middle fails to connect.

## Question 4: Part 3 Q1 – Present (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Make the two meanings explicit, for example: “All **people in attendance** should vote now. The gift is **a present (an object given to someone)**, not a person in attendance. Therefore the claim that the gift should vote now does not follow.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** A student may rewrite each occurrence in separate lines, replace the first sense with “here/in attendance” and the second with “gift,” or give two fixed versions of the argument. Any format is acceptable if it clearly removes the false appearance that one meaning of *present* links the claims and then states that the original conclusion does not follow.
- **Required meaning:** The response must perform both tasks in the prompt: (1) provide a fixed-meaning rewrite or explicit disambiguation, and (2) say that the original conclusion does not follow. Listing the two senses without rewriting or retesting is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly rewrites or disambiguates the attendance and gift meanings and correctly states that the original conclusion does not follow
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies both meanings but does not provide a usable fixed-meaning rewrite, or rewrites them but omits/obscures the retest judgment
- 0 pts: Keeps *present* ambiguous, says the gift should vote, says the original conclusion follows, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require two complete rewritten syllogisms, the labels “Sense A/Sense B,” or the exact model syntax; the student-facing prompt asks for a rewrite with fixed meanings, not a prescribed format.
- Accept “present = attending” and “present = gift” only for partial credit unless the response also turns that distinction into a rewrite/disambiguation and retests the conclusion.
- The fixed rewrite need not make every sentence true; its purpose is to expose that the locally intended meanings do not create one shared term.

## Question 5: Part 4 Q1 – Sound paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The shifting term is **sound**. In “sound argument,” it means logically solid, well-supported, or worthy of acceptance; in “microphones were sound,” it means functioning, working, or producing audible sound. Working microphones do not establish that the club's argument is logically strong, so the conclusion that the board should fund the trip is not supported by this word shift.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “good/valid/strong reasoning” versus “working audio equipment,” with any clear explanation that microphone quality does not prove the funding case. Students may phrase the main conclusion as “the board should fund the trip” or “the trip deserves funding.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must supply the shifting term, both relevant meanings, and why the shift fails to support the main funding conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies sound, accurately distinguishes logical quality from working audio, and explains why working microphones do not support the board-funding conclusion
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies two of the three required components, or gives all three with one materially unclear
- 0 pts: Focuses only on the janitor, cups, or assembly praise; treats working microphones as proof of a sound argument; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the technical definition of a sound deductive argument. “Good or well-supported argument” is sufficient for the first meaning in this context.
- Do not require exact quotations or discussion of the distractor sentences.
- “The microphones make sound” may identify the audio sense, but the answer still must distinguish that from the quality of the argument and explain the failed conclusion link.

## Question 6: Part 5 Q1 – Review: spirit week autopsy (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Conclusion:** the person who questioned the schedule is against school pride. **Apparent proof:** loyalty or moral pressure, either-or framing, confident “therefore” language, and/or a superficially tidy if-then form. **Defect:** the first premise falsely, doubtfully, or without support equates questioning one schedule with opposing school pride.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Ordinary-language descriptions such as “it shames disagreement,” “it makes only two choices,” or “questioning a schedule does not mean opposing pride” are acceptable when all three requested autopsy functions are present.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the actual conclusion, at least one source of apparent proof, and the unsupported bridge from schedule criticism to opposition to school pride.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Supplies all three requested pieces in recognizable and accurate form
- 0 pts: Omits one or more pieces, merely agrees/disagrees with spirit week, calls the argument formally invalid without locating the premise problem, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “bad inference,” “false premise,” “rhetorical force,” or “false dilemma.”
- A student may call the everyday leap a bad inference if the explanation correctly identifies the unsupported equation; do not require the claim that the printed if-then structure itself is invalid.

## Question 7: Part 5 Q2 – Review: songbirds (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Form test:** valid; if both premises were true, the conclusion would follow. **Fact test:** the first premise is false, doubtful, or overgeneralized because not all songbirds can fly long distances over open water. The second premise—that robins are songbirds—is true and is not the fact-test defect.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “valid form, false first premise,” “the structure works but the all-songbirds claim is false,” or ordinary language explaining that some songbirds lack the stated open-water flight ability.
- **Required meaning:** The student must state that the form passes/is valid and identify the universal songbird-flight premise as the factual problem.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Says the form is valid/passes and accurately identifies the all-songbirds open-water-flight premise as false, doubtful, or unsupported
- 0 pts: Calls the form invalid; identifies the true robins-are-songbirds premise as the main defect; gives no specific fact-test issue; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require “AAA,” “major premise,” “universal,” or “fact test.” Accept ordinary language that clearly separates valid structure from the false or doubtful first premise.
- A student need not supply a named counterexample, but an accurate counterexample may demonstrate the premise problem.
