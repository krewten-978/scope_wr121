# U1L12LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week12/lit-examples/U1L12LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L12LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L12LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 12 induction / evidence-ladder diagnosis to Cassius in *Julius Caesar* and Lady Macbeth in *Macbeth*.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Example:** a particular observed case offered as evidence.
- **General claim:** a broader conclusion about character, motive, or pattern.
- **Limited claim:** a narrower conclusion that fits the examples actually given.
- **Overclaim:** a conclusion larger than the evidence supports.
- **Counterexample / missing evidence:** evidence that would challenge or test the broader claim.
- **Revised claim:** a narrower conclusion that fits the examples actually given.
- **Evidence ladder:** observations/examples → pattern → limited claim → overclaim → revised conclusion.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Quotation is permitted but not required unless the student needs it to identify an example clearly.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Cassius's two examples (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Cassius uses (1) the Tiber swimming contest, where Caesar cried for help / nearly drowned, and (2) the fever in Spain, where Caesar shook / cried “Give me some drink” / showed physical weakness. Both are anecdotes about Caesar’s bodily vulnerability.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “swimming race / Tiber / Caesar called for help” and “fever in Spain / shook / sick / weak.” Accept brief paraphrase without scene titles. Accept “two stories of Caesar needing help / looking weak” only for partial credit unless both distinct episodes are identifiable.
- **Required meaning:** Both specific examples must be identifiable as the swim and the fever (or equivalent clear labels from the reader). A single vague “Caesar was weak once” is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names both examples accurately (ordinary paraphrase OK)
- 1 pt: Names one example clearly, or both only vaguely (“Caesar was weak”)
- 0 pts: Wrong episodes, pure plot summary with no examples, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require quotation or exact Shakespearean wording.
- Do not require analysis of political fitness on this item; that is later.
- Do not require the word “anecdote.”

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Limited claim about Caesar (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The examples support a **limited** claim that Caesar is physically vulnerable, not superhuman, and not literally godlike—or that Cassius has personally seen Caesar need help.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar can get sick / needs help / is mortal / is not a god / has ordinary human weakness / is not physically invincible.” Also accept “Cassius saw Caesar weak,” if framed as what the stories support.
- **Required meaning:** The claim must stay close to physical/human vulnerability or personal observation. Jumping to “Caesar must not rule,” “Caesar is a tyrant,” or “Brutus should kill Caesar” is an overclaim for this prompt (save that for Part 2).

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a narrow, evidence-fitting claim about physical/human vulnerability (or personal observation of weakness)
- 1 pt: Gives a partly narrow but vague claim, or mixes limited and overbroad elements
- 0 pts: Jumps directly to a broad political/assassination conclusion as what the examples alone support, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “limited claim.”
- Do not require students to deny all political implications; they must size what **these two examples** support.
- Accept concise answers; a full essay is not required.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Cassius's larger desired inference (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Cassius likely wants Brutus to infer that Caesar is unworthy of Rome’s awe, unfit to tower over other Romans, dangerous to honor as a ruler, or that Romans/Brutus should resist Caesar’s rise.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar doesn’t deserve worship,” “Caesar is not greater than other Romans,” “Caesar is unfit to rule,” “Brutus should not support Caesar,” “Caesar is a threat to Roman equality/honor,” or similar broader character/political inferences Cassius is pushing.
- **Required meaning:** Identify a **larger** inference beyond mere physical weakness—character, fitness to rule, or political stance. Restating only “Caesar got sick” fails the “larger claim” task.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies a broader character/political inference Cassius wants
- 1 pt: Vague “Caesar is bad” without political/character direction, or partly confuses limited vs larger claim
- 0 pts: Only restates physical weakness with no larger inference, wrong target, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require one exclusive political reading (unfit ruler vs resist rise vs not godlike leader); any text-grounded larger inference is fine.
- Do not require the word “overclaim” on this item if the larger desired inference is clear.
- Do not require proof that Cassius is sincere; the task is what he wants Brutus to infer.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Why examples are insufficient (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Physical weakness in two moments does not prove political unfitness, tyranny, or moral inferiority. A leader can be physically vulnerable and still competent; the examples may be cherry-picked; other evidence about judgment, policy, ambition, and public conduct would be needed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept any clear gap explanation: “two anecdotes ≠ unfit to rule,” “illness ≠ bad leadership,” “cherry-picked stories,” “need evidence of judgment/policy/character over time,” “Cassius may be biased,” “physical weakness doesn’t settle political worth.”
- **Required meaning:** Explain the mismatch between the sample (two physical anecdotes) and the larger claim (political/character unfitness). “Not enough evidence” alone is thin without naming that gap.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains the gap between anecdotes and broad conclusion with at least one concrete reason
- 1 pt: Says “not enough evidence” with little detail
- 0 pts: Treats Cassius’s anecdotes as decisive proof of the larger claim, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a fixed list of missing evidence types; one well-aimed gap is enough for full credit when clearly tied to the larger claim.
- Do not require dismissing the anecdotes as worthless; Week 12 values sizing claims, not ignoring examples.
- Do not require the term “cherry-picking” if bias/unrepresentativeness is clear.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Lady Macbeth's general claim (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Lady Macbeth claims Macbeth wants greatness / has ambition, but is too full of the milk of human kindness (or lacks the ruthless “illness”) to take the nearest way. In short: ambitious but not naturally ruthless enough.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “he wants the crown but is too kind,” “ambition without cruelty,” “he won’t do what it takes,” “too full of human kindness,” “lacks the illness should attend ambition,” or clear paraphrase of ambition + moral/kindness restraint.
- **Required meaning:** Both sides of her diagnosis must appear: desire for greatness/ambition **and** kindness / lack of ruthlessness that holds him back. Ambition alone or kindness alone is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Accurately states ambition/desire for greatness plus kindness / not ruthless enough
- 1 pt: Mentions ambition or kindness only, or both with one badly vague
- 0 pts: Wrong character claim, pure plot summary, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require quoting “milk of human kindness” if the kindness/restraint idea is clear.
- Do not require analysis of whether she is right; the prompt asks what claim she makes.
- Do not require discussing later murders on this item.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Visible and stronger evidence (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Visible evidence** in/near the passage includes Macbeth’s letter, the prophecy/title news, and Lady Macbeth’s intimate judgment of his nature from knowing him. **Stronger evidence** would include multiple actions or speeches across scenes showing hesitation, conscience, pursuit of ambition, or resistance to cruelty. Distinguish what is visible now from evidence known only later in the play when students use later knowledge.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Visible: letter content, Thane news/prophecy, her reading of his face/nature, his expressed hopes. Stronger: repeated choices over time, public deeds, how he handles Banquo/Duncan later (if labeled as additional/later evidence), other witnesses, behavior under pressure. Accept “we mainly hear her interpretation” as noting thin visible base.
- **Required meaning:** Both sides: (1) what currently visible evidence supports her claim and (2) what additional evidence would strengthen it. Plot summary without that evidence diagnosis is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names visible evidence and what additional evidence would strengthen the claim
- 1 pt: Only one side of the answer, or both sides very thin
- 0 pts: Plot summary without evidence diagnosis, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a fixed catalogue of later-play events.
- Do not punish students who carefully separate “known in this passage” from “known later”; that distinction is good method.
- Quotation optional; accurate reference is enough.
- Do not require the formal term “sample.”

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Revise Cassius's argument (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** A strong revision narrows the conclusion to fit the anecdotes, e.g.: “Caesar is not superhuman; Romans should not worship him as if he were above ordinary weakness,” or “These stories give reason to question Caesar’s godlike image, but more evidence is needed to judge his political fitness.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any revised conclusion that (a) keeps what the swim/fever stories actually show (mortal/physical weakness / not godlike) and (b) drops or hedges the leap to full political unfitness/tyranny without more evidence. Accept “Caesar can be weak like other men,” “don’t treat Caesar as a god,” “question the myth of invincibility,” plus optional “need more on leadership before deciding he must not rule.”
- **Required meaning:** The revised conclusion must fit the examples. Repeating a decisive anti-Caesar political verdict as fully proved by the two stories fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Revises to a narrower conclusion that fits the anecdotes
- 1 pt: Some narrowing but still overstrong, or narrow claim without clear link to the examples
- 0 pts: Repeats a decisive anti-Caesar conclusion without added evidence / as if fully proved by the two stories, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require one model sentence; grade the sizing of the claim.
- Do not require students to defend Caesar; only to match conclusion to evidence.
- A two-part answer (limited claim + “need more for politics”) is excellent.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Capstone evidence ladder paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** A strong paragraph explains that Week 12 helps readers value powerful examples without turning them into oversized claims. Cassius’s anecdotes and Lady Macbeth’s judgment are meaningful evidence, but students must ask what pattern they actually show, what evidence is missing, and how to revise a claim so it fits the sample.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any coherent paragraph that applies the evidence-ladder idea to **literary character claims** with nuance: examples matter; size the claim; note missing evidence; revise overclaims. Students may lean more on Cassius, more on Lady Macbeth, or both. Accept ordinary terms (examples, big claim, smaller claim, missing proof) without formal ladder labels if the method is clear.
- **Required meaning:** Must connect induction/evidence-sizing method to character claims and must not merely retell plot. Should show that examples are useful **and** limited—not “examples prove nothing” and not “one story settles character forever.”

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Applies the evidence ladder (or clear equivalent method) to literary character claims with nuance
- 1 pt: Thin application (names the method vaguely or only one play moment without claim-sizing)
- 0 pts: Plot summary only, rejects all example-based character reading without method, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all five formal ladder labels if the student clearly covers examples → limited claim → overclaim/missing evidence → revision.
- Do not require equal coverage of both plays; one well-worked literary application plus general method can earn full credit.
- Do not require quotation.
- Capstone is about method transfer, not a third close-reading of a new passage.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. Total: 8 × 2 = 16 points.
- Credit students who distinguish “supports a limited claim” from “proves nothing.” Week 12 is about sizing claims to evidence, not dismissing examples.
- Do not require identical wording to model answers; preserve logical force and text grounding.
