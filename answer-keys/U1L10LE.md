# U1L10LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week10/lit-examples/U1L10LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L10LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L10LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 10 irrelevant-proof / point-at-issue diagnosis to Brutus and Antony in *Julius Caesar*.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Point at issue:** the exact claim the speaker must prove for the argument to succeed.
- **Nearby claim:** a related claim that may be proved without proving the full conclusion.
- **Irrelevant proof / missing the point:** proof that answers a different or narrower question than the conclusion requires.
- **Partial proof:** evidence that supports only part of the case.
- **Repair or narrow:** add missing proof for the stronger conclusion or reduce the conclusion to what the evidence supports.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Credit students who distinguish “partly proves” from “proves nothing.” Week 10 is about proof-to-claim match, not dismissing all rhetoric.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Brutus's required conclusion (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Brutus needs to prove that killing Caesar was justified and/or necessary for Rome’s freedom (that the assassination was the right response), not merely that Caesar was imperfect, powerful, loved, valiant, or ambitious.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar’s death was justified,” “the killing was necessary,” “Romans are freer/safer because Caesar was killed,” “assassination was right for Rome,” or another clear paraphrase of the justification/necessity conclusion.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must state the assassination-justification conclusion at the full strength Brutus needs. Naming only ambition, love of Rome, or general disapproval of Caesar is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly states that Brutus must prove killing Caesar was justified/necessary (or an equivalent full-strength conclusion)
- 1 pt: Gets close with “Brutus must show Caesar was dangerous / bad for Rome” but leaves the death/justification conclusion unclear
- 0 pts: Gives only “Caesar was ambitious,” plot summary, Antony’s claim, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a quotation or the exact words “justified” and “necessary” if the full-strength conclusion is clear.
- Do not require students to evaluate whether Brutus succeeds here; the prompt asks what he needs to prove.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Nearby ambition claim (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Brutus tries to prove that Caesar was ambitious and that such ambition threatens Roman freedom or leads toward slavery. That is related to the assassination question but is not identical to proving that Caesar had to be killed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar was ambitious,” “ambition endangers liberty,” “living Caesar means slavery / dead Caesar means free men,” or “ambition deserves death in Brutus’s frame” when the student identifies this as the nearby claim Brutus pushes. Relation language may be ordinary (“connected but not the same,” “related concern,” “part of the case”).
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the ambition/freedom nearby claim Brutus works to establish. It need not fully analyze the proof gap (that is Part 2 Q1), but it should not treat the nearby claim as already identical to full justification unless the student clearly labels that as Brutus’s rhetorical pressure rather than completed proof.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies the ambition and/or freedom-slavery nearby claim Brutus tries to prove
- 1 pt: Mentions ambition or freedom vaguely without connecting it as the nearby claim in the speech
- 0 pts: States only the assassination conclusion, gives unrelated plot, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both “ambition” and “slavery/freedom” if one clearly captures Brutus’s nearby support path and the student is answering what nearby claim he tries to prove.
- Do not require the exact label “nearby claim” if the content is clear.
- The prompt does not ask for a full gap analysis here.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Ambition vs death gap (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Even if Caesar was ambitious, Brutus still needs to prove that the ambition was dangerous enough, imminent enough, and unmanageable enough (by lesser means) to justify killing him. Ambition may support caution, limits on power, or resistance without proving that assassination was necessary or just.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept ordinary explanations such as “ambitious is not the same as must die,” “he still needs proof of real danger,” “other responses than killing might work,” “ambition alone is too weak for death,” or naming a missing piece such as specific tyrannical acts, plans, or failed lawful remedies.
- **Required meaning:** The response must explain the gap between the ambition claim and the death conclusion. Mere dislike of Brutus or “he’s wrong” without the proof-match gap is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly explains why ambition, even if granted, does not by itself prove that Caesar had to be killed
- 1 pt: Expresses general doubt about Brutus or says “not enough proof” without naming the ambition-to-death gap
- 0 pts: Treats ambition as sufficient proof of death, gives plot only, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all three qualifiers (dangerous / imminent / unmanageable). One accurate statement of the gap is enough.
- Do not require formal terms “point at issue,” “nearby claim,” or “partial proof.”
- A student may note that Brutus also loads the slavery alternative; full credit still centers the ambition-to-death mismatch the prompt names.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Slaves/free men alternative (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The slaves/free-men alternative may prove a nearby point that slavery would be terrible and freedom valuable, or it may pressure hearers not to appear base. It risks assuming or skipping the still-needed proof that leaving Caesar alive means Romans die as slaves. Valuing freedom is not the same as proving Caesar’s continued life causes enslavement or that killing him is therefore justified.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “false/loaded choice,” “he makes them choose Caesar or freedom,” “it shames dissenters,” “it assumes Caesar equals slavery,” or “it settles the feeling before the proof” when the student explains the nearby-versus-full-issue split.
- **Required meaning:** The response must distinguish a nearby freedom/anti-slavery point (or pressure) from the fuller causal/justification claim still needing proof. Calling the line “emotional” alone is incomplete unless the missing proof point is stated.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes the valuable-freedom or pressure effect from the unproved claim that living Caesar means slavery / death is required
- 1 pt: Notes emotional pressure or a forced choice but does not clearly state what remains unproved
- 0 pts: Treats the alternative as full proof that Caesar would enslave Rome, plot summary only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require labels “false dilemma,” “loaded alternative,” or “irrelevant proof.”
- Do not require discussion of every later “base/bondman/Roman” line; one accurate account is enough.
- Accept answers that frame the defect as assuming the danger rather than as pure irrelevance, if the nearby-versus-issue distinction is clear.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Antony's smaller claims (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical models (choose any two):**
  - Captives’ ransoms filling the coffers → Caesar benefited Rome financially / brought public money home.
  - Caesar wept when the poor cried → Caesar showed compassion / was not made of only hard ambition.
  - Thrice refused the crown → Caesar showed restraint about kingship, or at least publicly hesitated to accept a crown.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Other accurate smaller claims tied to those pieces of evidence are fine (e.g., “refusing the crown weakens the simple ambition charge,” “ransoms show public benefit”). Students need not use all three evidence pieces—exactly two with accurate smaller claims satisfy the prompt.
- **Required meaning:** Two distinct pieces of Antony’s evidence must each be paired with what that piece actually supports, without jumping to the full “conspirators were entirely wrong” verdict as if each item alone proved it.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Two pieces of Antony’s evidence, each with an accurate smaller claim
- 1 pt: One accurate evidence-to-smaller-claim pair, or two pieces listed with only vague/overstated claims
- 0 pts: Plot-only list, invents evidence not in the passage, equates each item with full proof of total innocence, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all three reader examples. The prompt asks for two.
- Do not require quotations if the evidence piece is clearly identified.
- Mild overstatement on one pair with a solid second pair may still earn 1 pt rather than 0 if the student is clearly working the smaller-claim task.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Antony's narrower answer (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Antony’s evidence mainly answers or weakens the narrower charge that Caesar was simply ambitious in a tyrannical sense. It does not by itself fully prove every conspirator was morally wrong, every motive was corrupt, or that no political danger ever existed. It is powerful partial proof against the ambition charge and a crowd-moving case, not automatic complete proof of the largest possible verdict.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “mainly the narrower ambition charge,” “partial proof,” “weakens Brutus’s reason without settling every question,” or a careful “mostly narrower, though the crowd may take it as full proof.” A student may say Antony goes a long way against Brutus if they still distinguish the ambition charge from every larger moral/political conclusion.
- **Required meaning:** The response must choose or clearly lean toward narrower/partial versus full proof and explain the distinction. A bare “Antony proves Brutus wrong” without nuance does not fully answer the prompt’s or-choice.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes the narrower ambition charge (or partial proof) from a full verdict that the conspirators were wholly wrong / every issue is settled
- 1 pt: Says Antony mainly answers a narrower charge or only partly proves the case, but explanation is thin; or says “Brutus is wrong” with only slight nuance
- 0 pts: Treats the evidence as total proof of every larger claim with no distinction, says it proves nothing at all, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “partial proof.”
- Do not penalize students for noting rhetorical power or crowd effect if they still answer the proof-match question.
- Full credit does not require deciding Antony’s personal sincerity.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Repair Brutus's case (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical model with added evidence:** “Killing Caesar would be justified only if independent evidence showed specific plans or acts to destroy Roman liberty, imminent unmanageable danger, and that lawful or lesser remedies had failed—for example, documented abuses, orders, or preparations that could be checked.”
- **Canonical model with narrowed conclusion:** “Caesar’s ambition and possible rise gave Romans reason for vigilance and limits on power” (not “therefore assassination was justified”).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Many rewrites work. Added evidence may include specific tyrannical acts, credible plans, failed legal remedies, corroborated threats, or another independence-testable basis for death. Narrowing may stop at caution, suspicion of ambition, or concern for liberty without claiming justified killing. Wording need not match the models.
- **Required meaning:** The rewrite must keep a clear claim and either add missing proof for the strong death conclusion or narrow the conclusion to what ambition/freedom concerns can support. It must not keep “ambitious, therefore death” as a matched proof.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clear non-mismatched repair: added concrete missing evidence for the strong conclusion, or a genuinely narrowed conclusion that fits the weaker evidence
- 1 pt: Improves the case but leaves added evidence vague, leaves the claim unclear, or only partly removes the mismatch
- 0 pts: Still argues death from ambition/slavery labels alone, remains circular or mismatched, supplies no rewrite, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat models as examples, not required wording. One sufficiently concrete evidence category satisfies the add-evidence path.
- Do not require both adding evidence and narrowing.
- A student may conclude the strong death claim is not supported; that can be a valid narrowed/negative rewrite if structured clearly.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Capstone (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Week 10’s tool asks whether a powerful speech proves the exact point at issue or only a nearby claim. Brutus can be coherent and public-spirited about ambition and freedom while still falling short of proving that Caesar had to die. Antony can give moving, partly decisive evidence against simple ambition without automatically settling every larger verdict about the conspirators. The method lets a reader respect literary and rhetorical force while still testing proof-to-claim match.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Different paragraph orders and emphases are fine if they (1) use relevant speech/proof details from the passages or worksheet tasks, (2) explain respect for power/persuasion/partial truth, and (3) apply the point-at-issue vs nearby-claim test. Students may focus more on Brutus, more on Antony, or both, as long as the Week 10 tool is clearly applied to powerful speech versus completed proof.
- **Required meaning:** The paragraph must apply the Week 10 proof-match tool to these speeches and preserve the difference between force/coherence/partial proof and proving the right full point. Plot summary or a bare fallacy label is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Coherent paragraph that applies the point-at-issue / nearby-claim tool and explains how a speech can be powerful without fully proving the exact issue
- 1 pt: States the tool or distinction but with thin textual application, or applies it to only a fragment without the respect-vs-proof idea
- 0 pts: Plot summary only, treats rhetoric as automatic full proof or as worthless, keyword only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both speakers in equal depth if one is used well and the Week 10 distinction is clear.
- Do not require exact terms “point at issue,” “nearby claim,” or “prompt-match” if the concepts are clearly applied.
- Do not require quotation or the model’s sentence order.

## Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:
- Whole points only. Total: 8 × 2 = 16 points.
- Credit accurate “partly proves” judgments. Do not force every strong speech into “proves nothing” or every moving line into full proof.
