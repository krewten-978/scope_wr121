# U1L3LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week3/lit-examples/U1L3LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L3LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L3LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply the Week 3 Whately habit of identifying propositions, categorical form, quality, quantity, and distribution to Macbeth’s apparitions. Grade for logical translation and classification, not for plot summary.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

Use these terms consistently when evaluating student answers:

- **Proposition:** a claim that can be treated as true or false; commands and warnings are not propositions by themselves, though a proposition may be inferred from them.
- **Subject:** the class or thing the proposition is about.
- **Predicate:** what the proposition affirms or denies of the subject.
- **Quality:** whether the proposition is affirmative or negative.
- **Quantity:** whether the proposition is universal or particular.
- **Distribution:** whether a term is spoken of in its whole extension. In standard categorical forms: A distributes the subject only; E distributes both subject and predicate; I distributes neither; O distributes the predicate only.

Do not require students to use advanced terminology beyond the prompt if their classification is clear. Do require them to distinguish translation from proof.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Warning versus proposition (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical proposition-status answer:** “Beware Macduff” is not a proposition in the strict Week 3 sense. It is a command, warning, directive, or advice rather than a claim directly capable of being true or false.
- **Canonical inferred-proposition models:** Macduff is dangerous to Macbeth; Macduff may or will harm Macbeth; Macbeth has reason to be cautious about Macduff; or Macduff poses a threat to Macbeth.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any inferred statement may earn credit if it is a declarative claim that reasonably expresses the warning’s content. The student need not use a categorical A/E/I/O proposition.
- **Required meaning:** The response must both explain why the quoted warning itself is not a proposition and state one plausible proposition Macbeth could infer from it.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly classifies the line as a warning/command rather than a proposition, gives the truth-claim distinction in ordinary or formal language, and supplies one plausible declarative inference
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies either the status/explanation or a plausible inferred proposition but not both; or gives both while leaving the command-versus-claim distinction unclear
- 0 pts: Treats the warning itself as a standard categorical proposition without explanation, supplies another command instead of an inferred claim, or gives only plot summary

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal terms “imperative,” “truth value,” or “declarative.”
- “It is not a proposition because it tells Macbeth what to do; he might infer that Macduff is a threat” is sufficient.
- Do not require the inferred proposition to assert certainty. “Macduff may be dangerous” is a defensible inference from a warning.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Subject and predicate of the apparition’s claim (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical proposition-status answer:** Yes. “None of woman born shall harm Macbeth” makes a prediction or claim that can be treated as true or false.
- **Canonical subject:** Persons/people born of woman.
- **Canonical predicate:** Persons/people who will harm Macbeth.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “humans born from women” for the subject and “those destined/going to harm Macbeth” or “Macbeth-harmers” for the predicate. “Men born of woman” is acceptable when *men* is clearly used in the broad historical sense of persons/humans.
- **Required meaning:** The response must answer **yes** and identify the whole “born of woman” class as subject and future harm to Macbeth as predicate.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly identifies the line as a proposition and gives accurate, direction-preserving subject and predicate classes
- 1 pt: Correctly recognizes a proposition and identifies one term accurately while the other is vague; or gives accurate subject/predicate terms but omits the proposition-status answer
- 0 pts: Identifies Macbeth as the categorical subject, reverses subject and predicate, gives only plot summary, or supplies no accurate term classification

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “persons who will harm Macbeth”; a clear future-harm predicate is sufficient.
- Do not automatically accept “people able to harm Macbeth” as fully equivalent. “Able/can harm” is stronger and different from the apparition’s “shall/will harm” unless the student explicitly notes that Macbeth is interpreting the prediction as practical invulnerability.
- Macbeth is the object of the predicted harm, not the subject class of the categorical translation.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – E-form translation (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical translation:** No persons born of woman are persons who will harm Macbeth.
- **Canonical quality:** Negative.
- **Canonical quantity:** Universal.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “No people born of women will harm Macbeth” or another clear “No S are P” sentence preserving the born-of-woman subject and future-harm predicate. For quality, accept “negative” or an unambiguous statement that membership is denied. For quantity, accept “universal,” “all members of the subject class are covered,” “none,” or “no person,” when universal scope is clear.
- **Required meaning:** The response must provide an E-form universal-negative translation and separately state its negative quality and universal quantity.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a direction-preserving “No S are P” translation and explicitly identifies negative quality and universal quantity
- 1 pt: Gives a correct E-form translation but omits or mislabels either quality or quantity; or correctly identifies quality and quantity while leaving one translated class vague
- 0 pts: Does not produce an E-form translation, reverses or materially changes the claim, or omits/misidentifies multiple required components

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the student to repeat the letter E because the prompt already directs an E-form translation; the “No S are P” structure plus correct quality and quantity is sufficient.
- Do not withhold credit solely because the student uses “no/none/all are excluded” instead of the formal word “universal,” provided whole-class scope is explicit.
- Do not accept “Some persons born of woman will not harm Macbeth”; that O-form claim is much weaker.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Macbeth’s expanded A-form confidence (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical form:** A.
- **Canonical quality:** Affirmative.
- **Canonical quantity:** Universal.
- **Canonical overreach explanation:** Macbeth’s version assumes that every opponent he will meet belongs to the “born of woman” class and changes “shall/will not harm” into “cannot harm,” treating a prediction as practical invulnerability. It may also narrow the setting to battle while broadening Macbeth’s confidence beyond the exact dramatic wording.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** For quality, accept “affirmative/it includes all battle opponents in the cannot-harm class.” For quantity, accept “universal/all/every opponent.” For the explanation, accept any one accurate difference between the apparition’s words and Macbeth’s version, including the class-membership assumption, the “will not” to “cannot” strengthening, or the leap from a translated proposition to certainty/trust.
- **Required meaning:** The response must explicitly supply A form, affirmative quality, universal quantity, and explain at least one way Macbeth’s confident claim adds to or strengthens the apparition’s exact wording.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly supplies all three classifications and gives a specific, logically accurate overreach or added assumption
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies all three classifications but gives only a vague explanation; or gives a strong overreach explanation with one classification omitted or incorrect
- 0 pts: Omits/misidentifies multiple classifications, treats Macbeth’s confidence as word-for-word identical to the apparition’s statement, or gives only a trust/theme judgment

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not infer affirmative quality and universal quantity from the letter A on the student’s behalf; the prompt requests all three separately.
- Accept “all/every” for universal and “places opponents in the safe/cannot-harm category” for affirmative when the classifications are clearly separate.
- Do not require every model overreach. One accurately explained difference is sufficient.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Translating “only” (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical standard-form translation:** All persons who can harm Macbeth are persons not born in the ordinary way.
- **Canonical subject:** Persons who can harm Macbeth.
- **Canonical predicate:** Persons not born in the ordinary way.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “All potential Macbeth-harmers are people born non-ordinarily” or another clear universal-affirmative paraphrase that preserves the direction of **only**. Different class wording is acceptable if it retains both ability to harm and non-ordinary birth.
- **Required meaning:** The translation must make the people who can harm Macbeth the subject class and place all of them within the non-ordinary-birth predicate class. The subject and predicate must be stated separately or labeled unambiguously.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a correct direction-preserving “All S are P” translation and accurately identifies both subject and predicate
- 1 pt: Gives the correct translation but omits or unclearly labels one term; or correctly states the subject and predicate classes while leaving the standard-form sentence incomplete
- 0 pts: Reverses the implication, treats “only” as “some,” omits a central class condition, or gives no standard-form claim

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the letter A or the formal labels “universal” and “affirmative”; this prompt asks for the translation and chosen terms.
- Do not accept “All persons not born in the ordinary way can harm Macbeth.” That converse says every such person can harm him, which the source claim does not establish.
- Do not require the phrase “Caesarean birth” or any medical/metaphysical interpretation; “not born in the ordinary way” is the class specified by the prompt.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Distribution in the A-form translation (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical distributed term:** The subject, “persons who can harm Macbeth,” is distributed because the proposition speaks about every member of that class.
- **Canonical undistributed term:** The predicate, “persons not born in the ordinary way,” is undistributed because the proposition does not claim that every non-ordinarily-born person can harm Macbeth.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “all possible Macbeth-harmers are covered” for subject distribution and “only some or an unspecified portion of the non-ordinary-birth class may be Macbeth-harmers” for predicate non-distribution. “Not distributed” is equivalent to “undistributed.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must classify both terms and briefly explain the whole-subject versus non-exhaustive-predicate scope in the A-form translation.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly identifies distributed subject and undistributed predicate and explains both statuses by reference to the scope of the translated A proposition
- 1 pt: Correctly classifies both terms without a meaningful explanation; or correctly classifies and explains one term while omitting/misclassifying the other
- 0 pts: Reverses both statuses, gives no correct classification, or bases distribution only on word order rather than class scope

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a memorized phrase such as “A distributes the subject only” if the student’s explanation is conceptually correct.
- If the student uses a differently worded but logically equivalent A-form translation, grade the corresponding subject and predicate classes in that translation.
- A reversed, non-equivalent translation from Part 3 Q1 does not become correct merely because the student applies the A-form distribution rule consistently to it; full credit requires the distribution of the direction-preserving proposition.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Macduff and the subject class (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical class-boundary analysis:** Macduff was physically born from a woman’s body but delivered by an unusual surgical removal (“untimely ripp’d”). Therefore, whether he counts as “born of woman” depends on whether the phrase means all humans originating from women or only people delivered by ordinary childbirth.
- **Canonical logical effect:** If Macduff is outside the intended subject class, “No persons born of woman will harm Macbeth” says nothing that protects Macbeth from Macduff. If Macduff is inside the class, his harming Macbeth would make the prophecy false or reveal its wording as misleading.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “not clearly inside,” “not clearly outside,” “inside literally but outside the prophecy’s special/ordinary-birth sense,” or another defensible classification, provided the response explains the chosen class definition and its consequence for the proposition.
- **Required meaning:** The response must address the ambiguity in the subject-class boundary and connect Macduff’s membership or nonmembership to whether the universal claim applies to him.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains why Macduff’s birth makes class membership unclear and accurately states what follows under the inside and/or outside classification
- 1 pt: Recognizes that Macduff is an exceptional or disputed case but gives only a thin explanation of the class boundary or its effect on the proposition
- 0 pts: Gives only plot summary, insists “inside” or “outside” without defining the relevant sense, or says only that the witches lied without class analysis

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require one metaphysical, medical, or literary answer about whether Macduff was “really” born of woman.
- A student may defend a clear inside or outside answer rather than presenting both conditionals, but must define the phrase and explain how that classification affects the prophecy.
- Do not require quotation beyond the birth fact already supplied in the prompt.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Translating before trusting (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical categorical model:** “No persons born of woman are persons who will harm Macbeth” — E form (universal negative). A logical danger is that “born of woman” has an unclear class boundary, “shall not” may be strengthened into “cannot,” or translation may be mistaken for proof that the prophecy is trustworthy.
- **Canonical conditional model:** “If Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane, then Macbeth can be vanquished” or “Macbeth will not be vanquished until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane” — a conditional prediction, not one of the four categorical A/E/I/O forms without further reformulation. A danger is treating figurative or condition-laden language as an impossible condition and therefore as a guarantee of safety.
- **Canonical warning model:** The student may turn “Beware Macduff” into a proposition such as “Macduff is a threat to Macbeth,” while noting that the original warning itself is not a categorical proposition and does not prove the inferred claim.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any exact, passage-grounded proposition from the supplied prophecies may earn credit if the student (1) writes it as a clear claim, (2) identifies its form when categorical or accurately says that it is conditional/noncategorical when appropriate, and (3) states one specific logical danger of relying on the unclear dramatic wording.
- **Required meaning:** The response must contain all three requested components—proposition, form if possible, and one danger—and must distinguish clarification of a claim from proof of its truth or trustworthiness.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a clear passage-grounded proposition, accurately classifies its form or noncategorical structure, and explains one specific danger involving ambiguity, conditions, class scope, changed strength, or unjustified trust
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies two of the three required components; or supplies all three but leaves the proposition or danger too vague to show the logical issue
- 0 pts: Gives only plot/theme or moral advice, supplies no testable proposition, or treats translation itself as proof that Macbeth is safe and the speaker trustworthy

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the born-of-woman proposition specifically; the prompt allows a prophecy to be translated and tested, and the reader supplies multiple lines.
- If a student chooses a categorical proposition, require the correct A/E/I/O letter or an unambiguous equivalent form identification. If the student chooses the Birnam Wood conditional, accept “conditional/not A-E-I-O” rather than forcing a categorical letter.
- Do not require a particular danger from the model list. The danger must be logically tied to the chosen language, not merely “the witches are evil” or “Macbeth is overconfident.”

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. No half points.
- Grade only the components requested in each individual prompt; accept ordinary-language classifications when they unambiguously preserve the formal distinction.
- Do not reward long plot summaries unless they support precise logical classification.
- Total: 8 questions × 2 points = 16 points.
