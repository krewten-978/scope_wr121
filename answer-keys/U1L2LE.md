# U1L2LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 12

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week2/lit-examples/U1L2LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L2LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L2LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply the Week 2 Whately habit of defining terms and detecting ambiguity/equivocation to Macbeth passages. Grade for precise term work and logical explanation, not for plot summary or literary-theme naming.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

Use these terms consistently when evaluating student answers:

- **Term:** the important word or phrase whose meaning affects the reasoning.
- **Sense:** one specific meaning of a term in context.
- **Ambiguity:** a term has more than one possible meaning and needs clarification before reasoning from it.
- **Equivocation:** a term shifts from one sense to another inside an argument, making the inference look stronger than it is.
- **Inference check:** the student’s explanation of whether the argument still works once the term’s senses are separated.

Do not require students to use the word “equivocation” if they clearly identify the word-shift and its logical effect. Do require them to state the relevant senses in ordinary language.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Fair and foul as shifting terms (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical senses of *fair*:** (1) beautiful, attractive, pleasant, or outwardly appealing; (2) favorable, successful, honorable, morally good, or right.
- **Canonical senses of *foul*:** (1) ugly, filthy, bloody, stormy, or outwardly unpleasant; (2) unfavorable, corrupt, morally evil, or wrong.
- **Canonical danger:** Macbeth could wrongly infer that something favorable or attractive is morally good, or that something frightening or ugly is morally wrong/false. A day may be “foul” in weather, bloodshed, or appearance while “fair” in military outcome; those different respects do not prove a moral judgment.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any two plausible contextual meanings for each word may earn credit. Students may distinguish appearance from morality, weather from victory, pleasantness from rightness, or favorable outcome from ethical goodness. Wording need not match the model.
- **Required meaning:** The response must give two meanings of *fair*, two meanings of *foul*, and explain how confusing a moral sense with appearance or favorable outcome can support an unwarranted inference.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies all four requested meanings and clearly explains the logical danger of moving between outward/favorable and moral senses
- 1 pt: Supplies three accurate meanings; or supplies all four but gives only a vague explanation; or clearly explains the danger while leaving one word’s second sense underdeveloped
- 0 pts: Supplies two or fewer accurate meanings, merely summarizes the scene/theme, or treats all uses as one meaning without explaining a possible confusion

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a specific pairing from the model list. For example, “fair = pretty/favorable” and “foul = ugly/evil” is sufficient when the explanation distinguishes appearance or advantage from moral value.
- Do not require a claim that the witches’ line is a formal fallacy; the prompt asks how confusion **could** mislead Macbeth.
- “Fair means good and foul means bad” is too vague for full credit unless the student distinguishes at least two different kinds of good/bad meaning and explains the inferential risk.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Contradiction or different senses? (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical judgment:** The line does not by itself prove a strict contradiction.
- **Canonical explanation:** A contradiction would affirm and deny the same claim about the day in the same meaning or respect. Macbeth can call the day *foul* because it is bloody, stormy, ugly, frightening, or morally troubling and *fair* because it is victorious, favorable, successful, or promising. Those descriptions can both be true when they concern different respects.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “paradox,” “wordplay,” “layered meaning,” “not literally contradictory,” or “both can be true in different ways” when the student explains at least one defensible difference in sense.
- **Required meaning:** The response must answer the contradiction question and explain that different senses or respects can remove the apparent inconsistency.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Says the line need not be contradictory and explains, with a passage-relevant sense pair, how *foul* and *fair* can apply in different respects
- 1 pt: Correctly says the words may use different senses and gives a plausible pair but does not clearly connect that fact to contradiction; or states the logical same-respect idea without a concrete application
- 0 pts: Calls the line a contradiction merely because opposite words appear, says only that it is confusing, or gives no term-based explanation

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal phrase “same thing in the same respect.” Accept ordinary language such as “it was bad in one way and good in another.”
- A student may describe the day as militarily successful yet bloody, or promising yet morally disturbing; either contrast is defensible.
- Do not require the label “paradox.” The assessed understanding is why different senses prevent a strict contradiction.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Macbeth’s sense of man (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical term:** *man*.
- **Canonical Macbeth sense:** A morally responsible, honorable, properly courageous human being—someone whose courage remains within what is fitting or right.
- **Canonical limit:** Macbeth says he will dare everything that properly becomes such a person, but daring beyond that moral boundary does not make him more manly; it makes him “none” or less than the proper standard.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “honorable man,” “good/moral person,” “courage under self-control,” “a person who will not cross the line into murder,” or similar language. The limit may be stated as “courage does not require doing evil,” “murder is beyond proper manhood,” or “more daring is not always more courageous.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the term, define Macbeth’s ethical or bounded sense, and state the limit he places on daring.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly supplies all three requested components and shows that Macbeth distinguishes proper courage from reckless or immoral daring
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies Macbeth’s resistance to murder and either his sense of *man* or his limit, but leaves the other vague or implicit
- 0 pts: Says only that Macbeth is afraid/weak, defines *man* merely as adult male, or does not explain the moral limit in his statement

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact terms “ethical manhood,” “self-command,” or “moral restraint.”
- Do not require students to claim that Macbeth is permanently virtuous; the question asks what this line means, not whether his later actions remain consistent with it.
- “A real man knows when not to do wrong” is acceptable if connected to Macbeth’s limit on daring.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Lady Macbeth’s sense of man (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical Lady Macbeth sense:** To be a “man” is to be bold, daring, resolute, non-cowardly, and willing to carry out the promised murder.
- **Canonical shift judgment:** Yes. Her pressure depends on moving from Macbeth’s morally bounded sense of manhood to an aggressive/performance sense in which daring the deed proves manhood. Boldness or consistency with a promise does not establish that murder is morally fitting.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “brave enough to act,” “not backing out,” “keeping his word,” “tough/aggressive,” or “willing to take the risk” for her sense. Accept “equivocation,” “redefinition,” “ambiguity,” “she changes what *man* means,” or ordinary-language explanations of the shift.
- **Required meaning:** The response must state Lady Macbeth’s sense and explain whether—and how—her argument relies on a different sense from Macbeth’s.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Accurately states Lady Macbeth’s action/daring-based sense and explains that her pressure relies on shifting away from Macbeth’s moral-limit sense
- 1 pt: Recognizes that she equates manhood with courage or carrying out the murder but does not clearly explain the change in meaning; or identifies a shift without clearly defining her sense
- 0 pts: Gives only plot summary/insult, treats her accusation as proof that Macbeth should act, or never analyzes the meaning of *man*

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal word “equivocation.”
- Do not require students to decide whether Lady Macbeth sincerely believes her definition; the task concerns how her argument works.
- “She calls him less of a man because he is afraid” is incomplete for full credit unless the response also explains that this boldness-based definition differs from Macbeth’s morally bounded definition.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Done as physical completion versus settlement (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical senses:** (1) physically performed, carried out, or completed—the act of killing Duncan has occurred; (2) finished in consequences, safely over, morally settled, or free from future earthly/divine judgment.
- **Canonical explanation:** Completing the physical deed does not end guilt, retaliation, justice, political consequences, or moral/religious judgment. The first kind of “done” therefore does not prove the second.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept any two clearly distinct, passage-relevant senses such as “committed” versus “over with,” “the act is finished” versus “nothing more will result,” or “Duncan is dead” versus “Macbeth is safe from consequences.” The student need not discuss every possible consequence.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify at least two senses of *done* and explain why mere physical completion does not establish completion of consequences or judgment.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives at least two distinct relevant senses and clearly explains why the physical-completion sense does not entail final settlement
- 1 pt: Gives two relevant senses but only implies the logical gap; or clearly explains the gap while leaving one sense vague
- 0 pts: Gives only one sense, merely paraphrases the passage, or claims that once the murder is performed all consequences are necessarily finished

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require theological claims. Practical, political, psychological, moral, or judicial consequences are sufficient.
- Do not require every model sense; the prompt asks for **at least two**.
- “Done means finished” repeated twice is not two senses unless the student clarifies what is physically finished versus what remains unsettled.

## Question 6: Part 4 Q1 – Building and clarifying an equivocation (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical task:** The student chooses *fair/foul*, *man*, or *done*; writes a short reason-to-conclusion argument in which the chosen repeated term changes between two plausible senses; and rewrites it so those senses are explicitly separated.
- **Canonical model—fair:** “The prophecy is fair because it favors Macbeth. Fair things are morally good. Therefore the prophecy is morally good.” Clarification: “The prophecy is **favorable to Macbeth**, but being favorable is not the same as being **morally good**, so the conclusion is not established.”
- **Canonical model—man:** “Macbeth would be a man if he dared the murder. Men do what is honorable. Therefore daring the murder is honorable.” Clarification: “Lady Macbeth’s **bold enough to act** sense does not establish Macbeth’s **morally honorable person** sense, so the conclusion does not follow.”
- **Canonical model—done:** “The murder can be done quickly. Whatever is done is completely settled. Therefore its consequences will be settled quickly.” Clarification: “The physical act can be **completed** quickly without its consequences being **finished or settled**.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** The original construction need not match a model and need not concern the same exact claim from the play. It must use one permitted term in two genuinely different, plausible senses, and the apparent inference must depend on that shift. The rewrite may replace the repeated word, add sense-specific modifiers/definitions, or directly state the distinction.
- **Required meaning:** Full credit requires both products: an original equivocal argument with an identifiable reason and conclusion, and a clarified rewrite that makes the two senses visible. The rewrite must expose or remove the hidden shift; it need not make the conclusion true or produce a sound argument.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Provides both versions; the original contains a reason and conclusion whose apparent connection depends on one permitted term shifting between two plausible senses, and the rewrite clearly distinguishes those senses
- 1 pt: Attempts both versions and shows a relevant ambiguity, but the reason/conclusion structure is incomplete, the shift is only partly clear, or the rewrite does not fully separate the meanings; or gives a successful equivocal argument without the required rewrite
- 0 pts: Gives no argument, chooses no permitted term, shows no identifiable change of meaning, supplies only literary commentary, or gives an argument that fails solely for an unrelated reason

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not award full credit merely because an argument is invalid; its apparent support must specifically depend on the same word or phrase changing sense.
- Do not require a Shakespeare quotation, a model argument, formal notation, or the word “equivocation.”
- For the *fair/foul* option, students may focus on *fair*, *foul*, or a linked contrast involving both, as long as one repeated term changes sense inside the argument.
- A clarified rewrite that openly reveals the conclusion no longer follows fully satisfies the rewrite requirement.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. No half points.
- Grade the student’s term discipline and the components each individual prompt requests; do not impose a four-item template on questions that request fewer or different elements.
- Do not reward long plot summaries unless they support precise term analysis.
- Total: 6 questions × 2 points = 12 points.
