# U1L5LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week5/lit-examples/U1L5LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L5LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L5LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 5 formal-validity work to Brutus and Antony: mood, figure, middle term, and the difference between a formally valid map and proved evidence.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Mood:** the A/E/I/O sequence of the major premise, minor premise, and conclusion.
- **Figure pattern:** the placement of S, P, and M terms in the premises.
- **Form test:** the question whether the conclusion would have to follow if the premises were true.
- **Evidence test:** whether the premises, especially the pressure-point premise, are actually supported.

Do not require one exact formalization if the student's map is coherent and the form judgment follows from it.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Reconstructing Brutus (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical reconstruction:
- **Major premise:** All men/people whose ambition would enslave Rome are people who deserve death for Rome’s freedom.
- **Minor premise:** Caesar was a man/person whose ambition would enslave Rome.
- **Conclusion:** Caesar deserved death for Rome’s freedom.

Accept another coherent three-claim reconstruction when it preserves Brutus’s practical reasoning: a general principle connects dangerous ambition or enslavement of Rome to justified death/prevention, a Caesar-specific premise places Caesar under that condition, and the conclusion applies the death/prevention judgment to Caesar. Singular wording such as “Caesar was…” is acceptable.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies all three explicitly requested claims—a general major premise, a Caesar-specific minor premise, and a conclusion about Caesar—that share a stable linking category and jointly reconstruct Brutus’s argument.
- 1 pt: Captures Brutus’s general reasoning but omits or mislabels one claim, or gives three claims with an incomplete logical connection.
- 0 pts: Gives only plot summary, quotation, or separate themes with no recognizable three-claim argument.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model wording, quotation, or the exact phrases “enslave Rome” and “deserve death for Rome’s freedom.” Accept “destroy Roman liberty,” “make Romans slaves,” “should be killed,” or “should be stopped” when the three claims preserve the same logical jobs and strength.
- Do not accept a reconstruction that treats Caesar’s dangerous tyranny as already proved by the passage; this question asks for Brutus’s argument, not whether its premises pass the evidence test.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Mood and validity (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
For the canonical reconstruction, the mood is **A-A-A**: all M are P; all S are M; therefore all S are P. The specified form is **valid** because, if both premises are granted, the conclusion must follow.

Accept “AAA,” “A, A, A,” or “A–A–A.” If the student used a different coherent categorical reconstruction in Part 1 Q1, grade the mood according to the actual quantity and quality of those three claims. However, the worksheet’s separately stated all-M/all-S form is valid regardless of whether its premises are factually established.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly states the mood of the student’s three claims and says the explicitly stated all-M-are-P / all-S-are-M / all-S-are-P form is valid.
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies either the mood or the validity judgment but not both, or gives both with one classification inconsistent with the student’s own reconstruction.
- 0 pts: Supplies neither component correctly, calls the specified form invalid merely because a premise is doubtful, or treats validity as proof that Caesar was dangerously ambitious.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require hyphens, an explanation, or a factual judgment; the prompt asks for mood and formal validity.
- Do not force A-A-A if the student’s accepted Part 1 Q1 reconstruction genuinely uses different categorical forms. The mood must match that reconstruction, while the answer to whether the worksheet’s stated first-figure form is valid remains yes.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – S, P, M, and figure (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
For the canonical map:
- **S:** Caesar.
- **P:** people who deserved death for Rome’s freedom / the judgment “deserved death for Rome’s freedom.”
- **M:** men/people whose ambition would enslave Rome.
- **Figure pattern:** All M are P; all S are M; therefore all S are P, often abbreviated M–P; S–M; therefore S–P.

The required meaning is that S is the subject of the conclusion, P is its predicate or judgment, and M is the linking dangerous-ambition category appearing in the premises but not the conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly identifies S, P, and M and supplies the requested figure pattern in full-proposition or M–P/S–M/S–P notation.
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies at least two terms but omits or obscures the pattern, or gives a correct pattern with one term role unclear.
- 0 pts: Correctly identifies at most one role, merely lists Caesar/ambition/death without assigning S/P/M, or gives no recognizable figure pattern.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact wording of the model terms. Accept “likely enslaver of Rome,” “dangerously ambitious ruler,” or similar M language only when it performs the bridge from Caesar to the death/prevention judgment.
- The prompt explicitly requests both the three term assignments and the pattern. Do not award full credit for S/P/M alone.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Valid form versus proved premise (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
**No.** Brutus’s formal shape shows only that the conclusion follows **if** the premises are true. It does not establish the factual minor premise that Caesar was dangerously ambitious in a way that would enslave Rome. That premise still requires evidence; valid form and a proved premise are different achievements.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Says no and explains both sides of the distinction: validity concerns what follows if premises are granted, while Caesar’s dangerous ambition is a factual premise that still needs support.
- 1 pt: Correctly says the premise needs evidence but gives only a thin explanation, or accurately defines validity without applying the evidence problem to Caesar.
- 0 pts: Says valid form proves Caesar’s ambition, calls the form invalid solely because the premise is doubtful, or gives no relevant distinction.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the technical labels “minor premise,” “form test,” or “evidence test.” Accept ordinary language such as “the logic can fit together even if the claim about Caesar is unproven.”
- A correct “no” alone is incomplete because the prompt explicitly asks for an explanation.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Antony's evidence (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Antony’s three pieces of evidence are:
1. Caesar brought captives to Rome whose ransoms filled or enriched Rome’s public treasury/general coffers.
2. Caesar wept or showed compassion when the poor cried.
3. Caesar refused the kingly crown three times when Antony offered it at the Lupercal.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Lists all three explicitly requested evidence items, preserving the ransom/public-benefit, compassion-for-the-poor, and thrice-refused-crown meanings.
- 1 pt: Correctly lists one or two of the three items.
- 0 pts: Gives only Antony’s emotional effect, the “honourable man” irony, unrelated plot details, or none of the three evidence items.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require quotation, Shakespeare’s exact words, or the model order. Accept “made money for Rome,” “cared about poor people,” and “turned down the crown three times.”
- The prompt explicitly asks for three pieces, so two accurate items do not earn full credit.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Antony's logical role (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Antony does **not mainly give a cleaner formal syllogism than Brutus**. He mainly attacks Brutus’s minor/Caesar-specific premise by presenting counterevidence against the classification of Caesar as dangerously ambitious. His ransom, compassion, and crown-refusal examples make that premise less secure, even if they do not conclusively prove Caesar had no ambition.

Accept language such as “Antony tests Brutus’s evidence,” “he undermines the claim that Caesar fits the dangerous-ambition category,” or “his speech is less formally neat but puts factual pressure on Brutus’s premise.”

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies Antony’s primary role as attacking or weakening Brutus’s Caesar-specific ambition premise and explains how his evidence performs that role, while distinguishing it from merely presenting a cleaner syllogism.
- 1 pt: Says Antony challenges Caesar’s ambition or Brutus’s premise but gives little explanation, or discusses Antony’s counterevidence without identifying which part of Brutus’s argument it pressures.
- 0 pts: Says Antony’s main logical achievement is a cleaner formal syllogism without support, treats his reply as emotion with no premise-level relevance, or gives no comparison/explanation.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact term “minor premise.” Accept “the claim that Caesar was dangerously ambitious” when its role is clear.
- Antony’s irony may be mentioned as part of the attack, but irony alone does not satisfy the requested explanation unless connected to the premise and evidence.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Antony's possible syllogism (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Representative valid construction:
- **Major premise:** No people who refuse kingship and act for Rome’s public good are dangerously ambitious tyrants.
- **Minor premise:** Caesar was a person who refused kingship and acted for Rome’s public good.
- **Conclusion:** Caesar was not a dangerously ambitious tyrant.
- **Mood and validity:** E-A-E; valid in the stated first-figure form if the terms retain the same meanings.

Other constructions are acceptable. A full-credit response must present premises and a conclusion that form a genuine argument from Antony’s evidence, then give a validity judgment that matches the student’s own claims. If those claims have a clean categorical form, the mood must correctly classify them. If the student deliberately gives a weaker or non-categorical argument for which A/E/I/O mood is not available, the student may say so because the prompt says “if possible”; that explanation is not a missing answer.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies a plausible Antony-based syllogism with identifiable supporting premises and conclusion, correctly names its mood when the construction permits one (or accurately explains why it does not), and gives a matching conclusion-follows/validity judgment.
- 1 pt: Gives a plausible premise-and-conclusion map but omits or misstates an available mood or validity judgment, or lists relevant claims whose inferential connection remains incomplete.
- 0 pts: Gives only Antony’s evidence list, offers no conclusion, supplies a validity label unrelated to the constructed premises, or makes no syllogism attempt.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the E-A-E model, the strong conclusion that Caesar was not ambitious, or one exact interpretation. Accept a weaker conclusion such as “there is evidence against the charge of dangerous ambition” when the premises support that exact strength and the student judges the resulting inference coherently.
- Reject constructions that strengthen “some evidence against ambition” into “Caesar had no ambition whatsoever” without a premise strong enough to support that conclusion.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Stronger form versus stronger evidence (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical comparison: **Brutus gives the stronger or cleaner formal shape**, because his argument is readily reconstructed as the valid all-M/all-S first-figure form. **Antony gives stronger evidence against the pressure-point premise**, because his concrete examples challenge whether Caesar actually belongs in Brutus’s dangerous-ambition category. Formal strength concerns whether a conclusion follows from premises; evidential strength concerns whether those premises should be believed.

A nuanced answer may note that Antony can also be reconstructed into a valid form, but it must still compare the speeches as presented and distinguish his counterevidence from Brutus’s cleaner stated structure.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies Brutus as having the cleaner/stronger formal presentation, Antony as supplying the stronger counterevidence, and explains the form-versus-evidence distinction.
- 1 pt: Gives both canonical speaker judgments but does not explain the distinction, or makes a defensible qualified comparison while leaving one side unclear.
- 0 pts: Chooses only by sympathy, morality, emotional power, or preferred character; reverses the roles without a passage-grounded logical defense; or gives no form/evidence distinction.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact words “formal neatness,” “evidential strength,” or “pressure-point premise.” Accept ordinary language that clearly separates how well the claims connect from how well the crucial claim about Caesar is supported.
- Do not treat Brutus’s cleaner form as proof that his premises are true or Antony’s stronger evidence as conclusive proof that Caesar had no ambition.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. No half points.
- Total: 8 questions × 2 points = 16 points.
