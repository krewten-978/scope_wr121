# U1L7LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week7/lit-examples/U1L7LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L7LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L7LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 7's fallacy diagnosis—appearance of proof, false claim vs bad inference, emotional vs logical force, fallacy autopsy—to Lady Macbeth's pressure and Macbeth's settlement in Act 1, Scene 7.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Fallacy:** reasoning that seems to prove a conclusion but fails to prove it.
- **Appearance of proof:** language, tone, form, or pressure that makes a claim feel finished without earning it.
- **False claim:** an untrue, doubtful, or unsupported statement used as support.
- **Bad inference:** a leap from reasons to conclusion that does not follow even if the stated reasons are granted.
- **Emotional force:** the power of feeling (shame, fear, loyalty, desire) to settle a decision.
- **Rhetorical force:** skillful speaking, vivid images, identity pressure, and practical planning used to move a hearer.
- **Logical force:** whether the reasons, if granted, actually justify the conclusion.
- **Fallacy autopsy:** the requested conclusion, apparent proof, defect, and repair or narrowed claim.
- **Repair / narrowed claim:** a fairer statement limited to what the evidence supports, or a statement of what additional proof would be needed.

Grade for separating persuasion from proof. Plot summary alone does not earn points.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Conclusion pressed (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Lady Macbeth presses Macbeth to carry out the plan to murder Duncan—to stop hesitating and act on the enterprise he proposed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Macbeth must kill Duncan,” “he should go through with the murder,” “he must act rather than hesitate,” or the immediate identity-pressure conclusion that refusing or hesitating makes him a coward, less of a man, or untrue to his earlier desire. An identity formulation earns full credit when it clearly states what Lady Macbeth is trying to make Macbeth accept, rather than merely noting that she insults him.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must state a conclusion Lady Macbeth presses in Passage 1: either the demanded action or the manhood/cowardice judgment she uses to force that action.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States a clear action-conclusion about carrying out Duncan's murder or a clear identity-conclusion that hesitation/refusal makes Macbeth cowardly or less of a man
- 1 pt: Recognizes that she pressures him to act or prove himself but leaves the conclusion vague
- 0 pts: Merely says she insults, shames, or speaks to Macbeth; gives plot summary without a pressed conclusion; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the phrases “murder enterprise,” “prove himself,” or “you must kill Duncan.”
- Do not require a quotation. A faithful one-sentence paraphrase satisfies the prompt.
- Distinguish a conclusion from a pressure technique: “she attacks his manhood” alone describes a method, not what she is pressing him to accept.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Appearance of proof (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Any two concrete language moves that make Lady Macbeth's demand feel proved, such as questioning Macbeth's hope or love, calling hesitation fear or cowardice, defining manhood as daring the deed, contrasting earlier bold speech with present hesitation, using the cat image for desire without action, or applying intimate shame.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Quotations, close paraphrases, or accurate descriptions are acceptable. Other passage-grounded moves may earn credit if they identify distinct wording, images, accusations, contrasts, or identity pressures and explain or clearly imply how they push Macbeth.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify at least two distinct pressure moves in Passage 1. Two labels for the same move do not satisfy the stated count.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies at least two distinct, concrete pressure moves from the passage
- 1 pt: Identifies one concrete move, or gives only a general description such as “she shames him” without two distinct examples
- 0 pts: Gives no passage-grounded pressure move, substitutes plot summary, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require exact quotations or the model's terminology.
- Accept, for example, “she says he is afraid” and “she says doing the deed is what makes him a man” as two moves.
- Do not count “shame” and “emotional pressure” as two moves unless the student ties them to two distinct features of the language.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – False claim or bad inference (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Mainly a **bad inference**, often combined with a false, doubtful, loaded, or unsupported claim about what makes someone a man. Hesitation does not establish cowardice or lack of manhood, and neither a manhood accusation nor Macbeth's prior desire establishes that Duncan should be murdered.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** (1) **Bad inference** with an explanation of either leap; (2) **both** when the student distinguishes an unsupported manhood/identity claim from the leap to the murder conclusion; or (3) **false claim** as the main failure when the student specifically identifies and explains the false or unsupported premise that a real man must carry out this deed. Ordinary language such as “her reason does not prove it,” “she defines being a man unfairly,” or “wanting it before does not make murder right now” is acceptable.
- **Required meaning:** The explanation must identify a substantive defect in Lady Macbeth's pressure. Merely selecting one of the three choices is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Selects bad inference, both, or a specifically defended false-claim diagnosis and accurately explains the unsupported manhood premise and/or leap from pressure or prior desire to carrying out the murder
- 1 pt: Selects a plausible failure type but gives only a thin or partly accurate explanation
- 0 pts: Gives a choice with no explanation; says the speech proves the murder is right; or discusses only whether Macbeth is emotionally affected

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a named classical fallacy, the exact phrase “loaded definition,” or the formal terms “premise” and “inference.”
- “Both” earns full credit only when the student actually distinguishes a questionable supporting claim from a separate unsupported move to a conclusion.
- Do not accept “the murder is bad” alone; the prompt asks for diagnosis of the reasoning.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Emotional vs logical force (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Emotional force** is Lady Macbeth's shame, love accusation, manhood anxiety, fear, or cowardice pressure, which makes hesitation painful. **Logical force** would be reasons showing that murdering Duncan is justified or should be done. The exchange has strong emotional force but weak logical force because it moves Macbeth without establishing that the murder is right.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept ordinary-language contrasts such as “she makes him feel cowardly” versus “she gives evidence that killing Duncan is justified,” provided the response applies both sides to this exchange. A student may use a different concrete pressure from Passage 1 if the proof/persuasion distinction remains clear.
- **Required meaning:** Full credit requires both concepts, application to the exchange, and a distinction between moving Macbeth emotionally and actually proving the action should be taken.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Accurately contrasts emotional pressure with genuine justificatory support and ties both to the exchange
- 1 pt: Defines or illustrates only one side clearly, or gives a generally correct distinction with weak scene application
- 0 pts: Treats emotional effect as proof, gives plot summary only, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact labels “emotional force” and “logical force” if the two ideas are unmistakably distinguished in ordinary language.
- Do not require a quotation or every emotional technique in the model.
- A response that says only “emotion is feelings and logic is facts” is incomplete unless it applies that distinction to Lady Macbeth's pressure and the murder conclusion.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Cover-up plan (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The plan supports the practical feasibility and concealment of the crime: drugging Duncan's chamberlains, using or planting their daggers, staging blame, and performing grief could help Macbeth and Lady Macbeth commit and hide the murder. It fails to prove that murdering Duncan is morally right, necessary, justified, or otherwise an action Macbeth should take.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “it shows how they might get away with it,” “it makes success or concealment seem possible,” or “it reduces the practical risk” for what the plan supports. Accept “it does not show the killing is right,” “necessary,” “wise,” or “justified” for what remains unproved.
- **Required meaning:** The response must supply both requested parts and preserve the difference between a workable method for committing/hiding an act and a reason that justifies doing it.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly states what practical feasibility/concealment the plan supports and that it does not prove the murder is right, necessary, or justified
- 1 pt: Correctly states only what the plan supports or only what it fails to prove, or gives both with limited clarity
- 0 pts: Treats a workable cover-up as full justification; discusses only plot details without the support/proof distinction; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all listed plan details; one faithful description of feasibility or concealment is enough.
- Do not require moral vocabulary if the student clearly says that “can do or hide it” does not establish “should do it.”

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – “I am settled” (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Macbeth has gained **confidence that they can hide it**, together with resolve produced by Lady Macbeth's pressure; he has not gained proof that the murder is right.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “confidence in the plan,” “confidence they can get away with it,” “practical confidence plus emotional resolve,” or “he is persuaded/decided, not logically justified,” when the response explicitly denies that rightness has been proved.
- **Required meaning:** Of the worksheet's three choices, the correct choice is confidence that they can hide it. “Both” would claim that Macbeth has also gained proof that the murder is right, which the passages do not provide.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Selects confidence that they can hide it and explains that practical planning or emotional pressure settles Macbeth without proving the murder right
- 1 pt: Selects confidence and gives little explanation, or accurately describes resolve without clearly separating it from moral proof
- 0 pts: Selects proof that the murder is right or “both”; treats decision or confidence as proof of rightness; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “confidence that they can hide it.” Accept a clear equivalent such as “he thinks they can get away with it.”
- Emotional settlement and practical confidence may both be present, but that does not make the worksheet answer “both,” because “both” specifically includes proof that the murder is right.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Full autopsy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** (1) **Conclusion:** Macbeth should carry out Duncan's murder or stop hesitating and act. (2) **Apparent proof:** manhood or shame pressure, a love test, prior-desire language, and/or the later workable cover-up plan. (3) **Defect:** hesitation does not prove unworthiness; emotional pressure does not justify murder; and a possible cover-up proves feasibility, not rightness. (4) **Repair or narrowed claim:** for example, “Macbeth is afraid or hesitant,” “Macbeth previously desired the crown,” or “the plan may make concealment possible,” without claiming that murder is therefore justified. A repair may instead state what genuine moral or factual evidence would be needed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Different wording and order are acceptable. For each function, other passage-grounded answers may earn credit if the conclusion is genuinely pressed, the apparent proof is a real persuasive feature, the defect explains why that feature does not establish the conclusion, and the repair is narrower or better supported.
- **Required meaning:** All four numbered autopsy components requested by the prompt must be present and logically connected.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies all four distinct components coherently, with a repair that actually narrows or improves the unsupported conclusion
- 1 pt: Supplies two or three clear components, or all four with one materially incomplete component
- 0 pts: Gives plot summary without diagnosis; supplies one or no correct components; or repeats the murder/character conclusion as the repair

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model's sentence order, all listed pressure moves, exact quotations, or a specific named fallacy.
- A fair repair must limit the claim to what pressure or planning actually supports; merely rephrasing “Macbeth should murder Duncan” is not a repair.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Capstone paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** A speech can feel conclusive through shame, intimacy, identity pressure, confident language, and practical planning even when those features do not prove its central claim. In Passage 1, Lady Macbeth makes hesitation feel cowardly or unmanly; in Passage 2, the cover-up makes the crime seem feasible. Neither pressure nor feasibility establishes that Duncan should be murdered or that the act is right.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept different organization and ordinary vocabulary. The paragraph may emphasize other accurate details from both passages if it explains how those details create persuasive force and why that force falls short of proof.
- **Required meaning:** The paragraph must use relevant evidence from the Macbeth passages, explain why the speech feels settled or conclusive, and explain the remaining proof gap. A keyword or plot summary alone is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a coherent paragraph using relevant material from both passages to explain persuasive appearance and the failure to prove the murder conclusion
- 1 pt: States the appearance-versus-proof distinction but uses only one passage, gives thin evidence, or leaves the proof gap underexplained
- 0 pts: Gives plot summary only; treats Macbeth's persuasion or decision as proof; ignores the supplied passages; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact Week 7 terms “appearance of proof,” “emotional force,” or “logical force” unless the student-facing prompt demands them; accept ordinary language that clearly expresses those concepts.
- Do not require quotations, the model's sentence order, or every model detail.
- “She is persuasive” is insufficient by itself; the response must identify how the passages create that effect and why the murder conclusion remains unproved.

## Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:
- Whole points only. No half points.
- Total: 8 questions × 2 points = 16 points.
- Grade each question by its local semantic requirements above rather than a general synonym rule.
