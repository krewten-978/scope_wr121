# U1L11LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week11/lit-examples/U1L11LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L11LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L11LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 11 analogy / relevant-similarity diagnosis to Brutus in *Julius Caesar* and Macbeth in *Macbeth*.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Source case:** the comparison used to illuminate the argument.
- **Target case:** the person, decision, or situation being judged.
- **Relevant similarity:** the likeness that matters for the conclusion.
- **Disanalogy:** an important difference that limits the proof.
- **Overextension:** using a comparison to prove more than its likeness can support.
- **Narrow repair:** reducing the conclusion so it matches what the analogy can honestly support.
- **Decorative comparison:** a vivid image that adds force or style without carrying the proof load.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Credit students who admire literary force while still testing logical reach.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Brutus's source and target (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Source case:** a serpent’s egg / adder that will become dangerous if allowed to hatch or gain its sting. **Target case:** Caesar if he is crowned or allowed to rise to greater power.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “serpent’s egg,” “adder in bright day,” “snake before it hatches,” or “animal that grows mischievous as its kind” for source. Accept “Caesar,” “crowned Caesar,” “Caesar with more power,” or “Caesar before he becomes king/tyrant” for target.
- **Required meaning:** Both source and target must appear with correct roles. Swapping them is a material error. Students need not quote the full speech.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly names both source and target
- 1 pt: Names only one side clearly, or both with one role reversed/unclear
- 0 pts: Plot summary without the comparison mapping, invents unrelated cases, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both “serpent’s egg” and “adder” if one clear source image is identified.
- Do not require the labels “source” and “target” if the two roles are clear.
- Do not require the assassination conclusion on this item; that is Part 1 Q2.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Brutus's intended conclusion (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Brutus wants the comparison to support killing Caesar now, before Caesar becomes dangerous as a crowned ruler—preventive assassination / “kill him in the shell.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar must die now,” “stop Caesar before he is crowned,” “kill Caesar while he is still weak/unhatched,” “preventive killing,” or “Rome should destroy the danger before it grows.”
- **Required meaning:** The answer must state the strong conclusion the comparison is meant to support (death/stop-now), not only that Caesar might be dangerous someday.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clear preventive-killing / stop-Caesar-now conclusion
- 1 pt: Says Caesar is or will be dangerous without the kill/stop-now force, or leaves the conclusion vague
- 0 pts: Gives only plot, names only the egg image, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “preventive assassination.”
- Do not require students to judge whether the conclusion is proved here; the prompt asks what Brutus wants the comparison to support.
- Present- or future-framed paraphrases are fine if the death/stop-now claim is clear.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Persuasive similarity (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The relevant similarity is **potential future danger before full harm appears**: the egg is not yet harmful but will become harmful if it develops; Brutus fears Caesar may not yet be tyrannical but may become dangerous if crowned. The comparison also suggests danger is easier to prevent before it is fully grown.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “future danger,” “not dangerous yet but will be,” “power will add a sting,” “kill danger early,” “ambition grows into mischief,” or “easier to stop before the hatch.” One clear likeness that fits the preventive conclusion is enough.
- **Required meaning:** The response must name a likeness that could make the comparison persuasive for Brutus’s caution-to-prevention move. Mere “both are bad” or “both are snakes/people” is too thin.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names future/potential danger and/or preventive timing as the working likeness
- 1 pt: Mentions danger only in a thin way, or gives a likeness that barely connects to Brutus’s use of the image
- 0 pts: Gives no usable similarity, confuses similarity with the full death proof, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both “future danger” and “preventive timing” if one clearly carries the persuasive work.
- Do not require quotation.
- Do not require a disanalogy on this item.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Limiting disanalogy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** A serpent’s nature is treated as fixed and predictable in the image, while Caesar is a human political actor whose future actions are uncertain. Brutus even admits he has not known Caesar’s affections to overpower his reason. Unlike an egg, Caesar can deliberate, be checked by laws or politics, or act differently.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “people are not snakes” **when explained**, “Caesar’s nature is not known/fixed,” “humans can choose,” “laws or other Romans could restrain him,” “Brutus lacks present proof of tyranny,” or “an egg’s future is more determined than a person’s.”
- **Required meaning:** The disanalogy must limit the proof—showing why the egg image cannot by itself settle Caesar’s future or the need to kill him. A bare “different” without the limit is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a clear disanalogy that limits what the comparison can prove
- 1 pt: Gives a generic “people are not snakes” or “Caesar is human” with little explanation of the limit
- 0 pts: Treats the cases as identical, gives only plot, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require citation of Brutus’s “affections/reason” line if another solid human-vs-fixed-nature limit is stated.
- Do not require multiple disanalogies.
- Ordinary language is enough.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Judgment on Brutus's analogy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The analogy **supports a narrower warning** about the danger of unchecked or crowned power, but it does **not** by itself prove that Caesar must be killed. It becomes **overextended** if Brutus treats possible future danger as settled proof of necessary assassination without additional evidence. It is more than mere decoration because the future-danger likeness does some real work for caution.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “narrower warning,” “supports caution not death,” or “overextends if used for must-kill.” A “proves he must be killed” answer does not earn full credit unless the student invents extra evidence the analogy alone does not supply—and even then the prompt asks what the analogy proves. “Merely decorates” is usually too weak unless the student denies any future-danger likeness; prefer partial credit if they still notice vivid fear without proof.
- **Required meaning:** The response must choose among whole claim / narrower warning / decoration (or clear equivalent) and explain the proof limit. A label alone is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Balanced judgment: analogy supports a warning/caution about power but not full proof that Caesar must be killed
- 1 pt: Says the analogy is good or bad / overextended with thin explanation, or chooses a category without clear proof-limit reasoning
- 0 pts: Treats the analogy as automatically proving killing is necessary, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact terms “overextension” or “decorative comparison.”
- Do not penalize respect for the image’s literary force if the logical limit is clear.
- Canonical category is narrower warning; other labels need explanations that preserve that logical force.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Narrow rewrite (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical models:** “If Caesar is crowned, Romans should be cautious about concentrating unchecked power.” / “Caesar’s rise gives reason to set safeguards before granting more authority.” / “Possible future danger warrants vigilance, not automatic death, unless additional evidence of imminent tyranny is supplied.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Many narrow rewrites work: caution, safeguards, delay of crown, legal checks, watchfulness, or “death only if independent evidence shows unavoidable tyranny.” Students may keep a conditional stronger claim if they explicitly require added evidence. Wording need not match the models.
- **Required meaning:** The rewrite must lower the conclusion to what the analogy can honestly support, or state what extra evidence would be required before death is justified. Repeating “therefore kill him” without added evidence fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Rewrites to a narrower caution/safeguards conclusion, or keeps a strong claim only with clearly named needed added proof
- 1 pt: Some narrowing but still overstrong, or names “need more proof” without a usable rewritten claim
- 0 pts: Repeats must-kill from the egg image alone, supplies no rewrite, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat models as examples, not required wording.
- Do not require a fixed number of safeguards or evidence items; one clear narrowed claim or one concrete added-proof category is enough.
- A student may conclude preventive death is not justified by the analogy; that is a valid narrow/negative rewrite if clear.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Macbeth's bear-like comparison (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Source case:** a bear tied to a stake / baited bear forced to fight the course. **Target case:** Macbeth near defeat. **Relevant similarity:** surrounded or trapped, cannot easily flee, must fight under violent pressure. **Disanalogy:** Macbeth is a moral agent whose earlier choices helped create the trap; unlike a baited animal, he can still be judged for choices and motives, or he is not literally without alternatives in the same way an animal is.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “bear at bay,” “tied animal,” or “beast forced to fight” for source; “Macbeth,” “Macbeth in Act 5,” or “Macbeth facing enemies” for target. Similarity may focus on trapped feeling, no flight, forced combat. Disanalogy may focus on agency, guilt, prior choices, human planning, or moral responsibility.
- **Required meaning:** All four components the prompt requests must appear: source, target, relevant similarity, and one disanalogy. Swapping source/target is a material error.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Includes source, target, relevant similarity, and one limiting disanalogy
- 1 pt: Includes two or three of the four elements clearly, or all four with one weak/off-target
- 0 pts: Plot summary only, misses the comparison mapping, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require quotation of “fight the course.”
- Do not require a final no-choice judgment here; that is Part 4 Q2.
- One solid disanalogy is enough.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Macbeth's moral choice (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The comparison **mainly describes** Macbeth’s sense of being trapped and forced into combat. It does **not prove** that he has no moral choice left or no responsibility. The image is psychologically powerful but should not erase agency, prior choices, or remaining decisions about how to act.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “describes feeling trapped,” “explains desperation,” “vivid self-picture not proof of zero agency,” or “shows pressure without removing responsibility.” A nuanced answer may say he has narrowed options in battle while still denying that the analogy proves total moral non-responsibility.
- **Required meaning:** The response must take the prompt’s choice (no moral choice left vs mainly describes being trapped) and explain why the likeness does or does not carry full proof of no agency. Treating the image as automatic proof of no choice fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes vivid trapped self-description from proof that Macbeth has no moral agency/responsibility left
- 1 pt: Thin answer that he feels trapped, or leans the right way without clear proof-limit explanation
- 0 pts: Says the analogy proves Macbeth has no choice without qualification, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “agency,” “overextension,” or “decorative comparison.”
- Do not require a full moral theory of the play; the analogy-limit is enough.
- Credit students who note literary force while denying full proof of no choice.

## Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:
- Whole points only. Total: 8 × 2 = 16 points.
- Credit students who admire the literary force of the comparisons while still testing their logical reach.
- Sample similarities and disanalogies are examples, not exclusive lists.
