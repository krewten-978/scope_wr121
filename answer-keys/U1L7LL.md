## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Fallacy:** reasoning that seems to prove a conclusion but fails to prove it.
- **False claim:** an untrue statement used in the argument. A student may also identify a premise as doubtful, unsupported, or an unjustified generalization when the printed example does not establish its truth.
- **Bad inference:** a leap from reasons to conclusion that does not follow, even if the stated reasons are granted.
- **Appearance of proof:** the surface look of support (for example, confident form, moral pressure, loyalty language, or crowd pressure) without earned proof.
- **Fallacy autopsy:** diagnosis of the requested conclusion, apparent proof, defect, and—when requested—repair or narrowed claim.
- **Form test:** if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to follow?
- **Fact test:** are the premises true, false, doubtful, or unproved?
- **Undistributed middle:** a formal flaw in which the middle term never covers its whole class, so the extremes are not forced together.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Transfer students (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The main failure is a **false claim** or failed fact test: “All transfer students skip advisory” is an unsupported and implausible universal generalization.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “false premise,” “doubtful premise,” “unsupported assumption/generalization,” or an explanation that the argument gives no basis for saying every transfer student skips advisory.
- **Required meaning:** The response must locate the defect in the truth or support of the universal premise. The categorical inference itself is valid if both premises are granted, so “bad inference” or “both” is not correct merely because a false premise cannot establish a sound conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies the false/doubtful/unsupported universal premise as the main failure and briefly explains why the all-transfer-students claim fails the fact test
- 1 pt: Points to the first premise or calls it an overgeneralization but gives an incomplete explanation
- 0 pts: Says only that Maya skips or does not skip advisory; calls the form invalid; selects “bad inference” or “both” without identifying a separate inference defect; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “false claim,” “major premise,” “universal,” or “fact test” if the student's ordinary language clearly identifies the unsupported all-transfer-students premise.
- Do not treat a false premise as, by itself, a bad inference. The conclusion follows conditionally from the two printed premises even though the argument is not sound.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Devon and learning (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The main failure is a **bad inference**. Devon's opposition to one phone policy does not establish that Devon does not care about learning.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “does not follow,” “unsupported conclusion,” “unfair leap,” or an explanation that someone can care about learning while disagreeing with this policy.
- **Required meaning:** The response must distinguish policy disagreement from lack of concern for learning. The truth of the conclusion about Devon is not supplied, so the defect is the attempted inference, not a demonstrably false conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies the bad inference in ordinary or technical language and explains the leap from opposing the policy to not caring about learning
- 1 pt: Says the argument is weak or unsupported but does not clearly describe the leap
- 0 pts: Treats the conclusion as forced; merely asserts that Devon does or does not care; identifies only an unrelated factual error; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “bad inference.” Accept “does not follow,” “the reason does not prove the conclusion,” and comparable language when the policy/learning distinction is clear.
- Do not require a separate formal fallacy name.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Spirit week (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The argument appears strong because it uses a tidy if-then pattern plus moral or loyalty pressure and a confident “therefore.” It fails because the first premise unjustifiably equates questioning a schedule with opposing school pride; that premise is false, doubtful, or unsupported.
- **Acceptable alternatives for the appearance:** moral accusation, loyalty language, either/or framing, social shame, confident wording, or the argument's superficially neat structure.
- **Acceptable alternatives for the defect:** unsupported generalization, false/doubtful first premise, false dilemma, overclaim, or—in ordinary language—an unjustified leap from questioning one schedule to opposing school pride.
- **Required meaning:** Full credit requires one specific feature that creates apparent strength and an explanation that questioning the schedule does not establish opposition to school pride.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies at least one specific source of apparent strength and accurately explains the unsupported equation between schedule questions and opposition to school pride
- 1 pt: Clearly identifies only the appearance or only the defect
- 0 pts: Merely agrees or disagrees with spirit week; says only that the conclusion is false without diagnosing the reasoning; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Accept emotional force, rhetorical force, moral pressure, loyalty pressure, confident wording, or neat form as the appearance of strength.
- A student may call the everyday leap a “bad inference” if the explanation correctly locates the unsupported bridge between questioning the schedule and opposing school pride. Do not require or reward the claim that the printed if-then form is formally invalid; if its first premise were granted, the conclusion would follow.

## Question 4: Part 3 Q1 – Real teammate autopsy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** (1) **Conclusion:** the person who voted against overnight camp is not a real teammate. (2) **Apparent proof:** a loyalty/identity test, backed by a valid-looking if-then pattern, treats the vote as proof of character. (3) **Defect:** the first premise uses a false, stacked, or unsupported definition of “real teammate”; one vote about camp does not settle a person's overall loyalty or character. (4) **Repair or narrowed claim:** for example, “You disagree with overnight training camp,” “Voting against camp may show a disagreement about this training plan,” or “We should debate the plan on its merits.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Other wording is acceptable if it supplies all four requested functions. A different repair may earn credit if it removes the character-expulsion claim and makes only a conclusion supported by the vote, or states what further evidence would be needed.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the actual conclusion, describe what makes the argument seem persuasive, diagnose the unfair real-teammate test, and provide a genuinely narrower or better-supported replacement.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: All four requested autopsy components are present, distinguishable, and logically coherent
- 1 pt: Two or three components are clear, or all four appear but one is materially incomplete
- 0 pts: One or no components are correct; the response accepts the character judgment as proved; or the proposed repair merely repeats “not a real teammate”

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the four components in the model's sentence order or require the terms “stacked definition,” “conditional,” or “character expulsion.”
- Accept concise labels, numbered answers, or prose when all four requested ideas can be observed.
- A repair must change the unsupported character judgment; merely softening its tone without narrowing the claim is incomplete.

## Question 5: Part 4 Q1 – Dress code paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Conclusion:** the new dress code must be accepted without debate, or objectors should stop opposing it. **Apparent proof:** the president invokes loyalty, claims every loyal student already agrees, labels objectors troublemakers, and/or treats silence or sitting down as proof of loyalty. **Defect:** those pressures do not establish that the policy is right or that debate is illegitimate; they substitute a loyalty test and social pressure for relevant support.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept either central conclusion stated above or a faithful combination of them. One accurately identified source of apparent proof is enough; students need not list every device. Accept “peer pressure,” “shaming,” “false choice,” “unsupported loyalty claim,” “irrelevant character attack,” or comparable language when the explanation shows why the proof does not reach the conclusion.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must identify the dress-code/stop-opposing conclusion, at least one actual reason or pressure used to make it seem proved, and the gap between that pressure and the policy conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a faithful main conclusion, at least one specific source of apparent proof from the president's speech, and an accurate defect
- 1 pt: Gives two of the three requested autopsy components clearly, or partially identifies all three
- 0 pts: Treats cafeteria noise, soup, or the teacher's request as the main argument; supplies only a personal opinion about dress codes; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a quotation, all four model details, a particular fallacy label, or the exact model wording.
- A paraphrase is acceptable if it preserves the conclusion that debate/opposition should end and ties the defect to loyalty, silence, shaming, or another printed pressure.
- The cafeteria noise, soup joke, and teacher's seating request are distractors, not support for the dress-code conclusion.

## Question 6: Part 5 Q1 – Review: songbirds form/fact (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Form test:** valid AAA form; if both premises were true, the conclusion would follow. **Fact test:** the first premise is false, doubtful, or overgeneralized because not all songbirds can fly long distances over open water. The second premise—that robins are songbirds—is true and is not the fact-test defect.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “valid form, false first premise,” “the structure works but the all-songbirds claim is false,” or ordinary language explaining that some songbirds lack the stated long-distance open-water ability.
- **Required meaning:** The response must say that the form passes or is valid and must identify the universal songbird-flight premise as the factual problem.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: States that the form is valid/passes and accurately identifies the all-songbirds open-water-flight premise as false, doubtful, or unsupported
- 0 pts: Calls the form invalid; identifies the true robins-are-songbirds premise as the main defect; gives no specific fact-test problem; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “AAA,” “major premise,” “universal,” or “fact test.” Accept ordinary language that clearly separates “the structure works” from “the first claim about all songbirds is factually wrong or doubtful.”
- A student need not supply a named counterexample, but an accurate counterexample may demonstrate the premise problem.

## Question 7: Part 5 Q2 – Review: maples oaks (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Invalid** because the middle term “trees” is undistributed: both maples and oaks are placed within the larger class of trees, but that shared membership does not connect all oaks to maples.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “undistributed middle,” “the premises only show that both are trees,” “two groups can belong to the same larger group without being the same group,” or a relevant counterexample such as two different kinds of tree.
- **Required meaning:** The response must classify the argument as invalid and explain that shared membership in the tree class does not force oaks to be maples.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Says invalid/not formally valid and gives the undistributed-middle idea in technical or clear ordinary language
- 0 pts: Gives only “invalid” with no explanation; says valid; reverses the relationship; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact term “undistributed middle.”
- Do not award the point for “invalid” alone because the prompt explicitly asks for a brief explanation.
