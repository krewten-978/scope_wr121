## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Testimony:** a claim received from another person, witness, record, report, or source.
- **Authority:** a source treated as worth hearing because of office, training, experience, or access.
- **Credibility:** the degree to which a source deserves trust in this case.
- **Access:** whether the source was in a position to know the fact claimed.
- **Relevant expertise:** training or knowledge that actually matches the question being judged.
- **Bias / pressure:** a motive or pressure that may tilt a source toward one answer.
- **Corroboration:** support from independent sources, records, or evidence.
- **Careful conclusion:** a trust judgment stated at the right strength: possible, reasonable, strongly supported, contradicted, or needing more investigation.
- **Review terms:** prior-week skills only: Week 12 induction/overclaim/revised claim and Week 11 analogy source/target/relevant similarity.

Week 13 is not “trust no one”; it is matching trust to access, expertise, bias, and corroboration. Credit careful probability language.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Debate club adviser (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Claim:** the debate club will move to Wednesdays. **Source:** the club adviser (as cited by the student newspaper). **Access:** strong / relevant access because the adviser approves or knows the club calendar. The newspaper report is more credible if it accurately quotes or names the adviser.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Claim: “debate moves to Wednesdays,” “calendar change to Wednesday.” Source: “adviser,” “club adviser named in the paper,” or “newspaper citing the adviser” (distinguish newspaper as channel vs adviser as knowing source). Access: “yes, the adviser knows/approves the calendar,” “good access,” “in a position to know.”
- **Required meaning:** All three prompt elements: claim, source, and whether the source has access. Treating the newspaper alone as the ultimate knower without the adviser, or ignoring access, is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies claim, source, and strong/relevant access
- 1 pt: Identifies two of the three elements, or all three with one materially vague
- 0 pts: Treats the report as unsupported rumor ignoring the adviser, invents no-access without reason, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal words “testimony” or “credibility.”
- Do not require a full newspaper-accuracy investigation; noting that the paper cites the adviser is enough.
- Accept ordinary language for access (“the adviser would know”).

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Robotics rumor (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The student who skipped the meeting can directly know only that **they heard** a report or rumor. The **cancellation itself** needs a better source (coach/adviser, team officer, official email, calendar update, or someone who attended).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Direct knowledge: “heard a claim,” “secondhand report,” “not at the meeting.” Better source: coach, adviser, captain, official message, attendee, posted cancellation. Accept “hearsay” or “rumor” as naming the access problem.
- **Required meaning:** Separate what the skipping student can know directly from the trip-cancellation fact that needs a better source. Accepting the rumor as direct knowledge of cancellation fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Separates direct knowledge from the claim needing a better source
- 1 pt: Says “needs confirmation” without explaining access, or only one side clear
- 0 pts: Accepts the rumor as direct knowledge of cancellation, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a specific named better source type; one clear better-source idea is enough.
- Do not require proving the trip was or was not cancelled.
- Do not require the word “access” if the position-to-know problem is clear.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Musician and phone security (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The musician has fame, product-endorsement influence, or perhaps ordinary user experience—not automatic cybersecurity expertise. Relevant expertise would come from security engineers, independent technical reviews, encryption audits, vulnerability records, or specialists who can test the phone’s security.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Authority the musician has: celebrity, popularity, brand deal, maybe personal use. Relevant expertise: cybersecurity experts, engineers, independent labs, technical reviews, hackers/researchers testing security. Accept “famous ≠ security expert.”
- **Required meaning:** Distinguish celebrity/endorsement authority from relevant technical expertise for the security claim. Treating fame as sufficient security proof fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes celebrity/endorsement authority from relevant technical expertise
- 1 pt: Vaguely says the musician is not an expert without naming what expertise would matter
- 0 pts: Treats fame as sufficient security proof, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a technical list of security tests; one clear relevant-expert type is enough.
- Do not require claiming the phone is insecure; the task is source relevance.
- Do not require the formal term “authority” if roles are clear.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Nurse and council president (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** For the **health** question, the nurse has more relevant expertise and professional access to health guidance. The student council president may care about event attendance but does not have equal authority on fever transmission or staying home. A careful answer can include both concerns while giving the health judgment more weight on the health issue.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “nurse is more relevant for illness/stay-home,” “president speaks to events/attendance, not medical risk,” “different questions,” “health expertise vs event logistics.” Students may note the president’s concern is real but off the health question.
- **Required meaning:** Compare relevance **for the health question** and prefer the nurse on that question. Treating attendance concern as equal medical expertise fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Compares relevance of the two sources accurately for the health question
- 1 pt: Chooses the nurse but gives little explanation, or explains weakly
- 0 pts: Treats event attendance concern as equal medical expertise, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require dismissing the president’s attendance concern as worthless; it is a different issue.
- Do not require clinical credentials beyond “school nurse / health role.”
- Ordinary language is enough.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Restaurant inspection score (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The restaurant owner has a **business incentive** to present the score favorably, and the visible sign may be **outdated** (last year’s score). Corroboration could include the current inspection report, health department website, dated certificate, or independent public record. Bias does not prove the owner is lying, but the claim needs current evidence.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Bias/pressure: profit, reputation, wanting customers, incentive to look clean. Corroboration: current score, health dept site, dated report, independent inspection record, recent certificate. Accept noting the year mismatch as a key problem.
- **Required meaning:** Both (1) bias or pressure (or outdated-sign problem tied to reliability) and (2) specific corroboration that would help. Accepting last year’s sign as proof of a current excellent score fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names pressure/bias (or clear reliability pressure) and specific corroboration
- 1 pt: Mentions bias or corroboration only, or both very thinly
- 0 pts: Accepts last year’s sign as proof of the current claim, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require accusing the owner of lying; incentive + need for current record is enough.
- Do not require multiple corroboration types; one clear independent check is enough.
- Do not require the word “bias” if “reason to spin the score” is clear.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Group chat bus report (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** This is **not** independent corroboration if all three students rely on the same group chat message. It would be stronger with a bus-tracking app, school transportation notice, timestamped photo, driver report, or separate witnesses who directly observed the late bus.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Not independent: “same source three times,” “echo chamber,” “one message repeated,” “not three witnesses.” Stronger: independent observers, official notice, tracking data, photo/video with time, driver/dispatch, students who saw the bus themselves separately.
- **Required meaning:** Explain the shared-source problem and give at least one stronger corroboration path. Counting three repetitions as three independent sources fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains the shared-source problem and gives stronger corroboration
- 1 pt: Says “not independent” without details, or only one side
- 0 pts: Counts three repetitions as three independent sources, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require proving the bus was late or on time.
- Do not require both app and photo; one stronger independent path is enough.
- Accept “they all read the same text” as the core diagnosis.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Shuttle credibility map (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer (map elements):**
  - **Claim(s):** company: ridership strong enough to expand; association: shuttle mostly empty.
  - **Sources:** shuttle company; neighborhood association volunteers; city transit office (fare-card data not fully released).
  - **Access / expertise:** company has operating/boarding data but may count transfers in a self-serving way; volunteers have direct but narrow observation (one rainy Saturday morning); transit office six-month fare-card data is broader if released.
  - **Bias:** company benefits from expansion; association may oppose service or overgeneralize from a tiny sample.
  - **Corroboration / conflict:** claims conflict; both incomplete; official summary not yet public.
  - **Careful conclusion:** expansion or cancellation is not settled; wait for full fare-card summary and clarify counting methods / gather better observation windows.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Ordinary-language maps that hit the same substance. Students may structure as a paragraph or labeled list. Careful conclusion may be “need the transit data,” “neither side proved,” “don’t expand or cancel yet,” or “resolve counting method first.”
- **Required meaning:** The prompt asks for claim, source, access, expertise, possible bias, corroboration or conflict, and careful conclusion. A response that only picks a side without credibility analysis fails. Minor label variation is OK if contents cover these jobs.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Includes source analysis covering access/expertise, bias, corroboration/conflict, and a careful (non-absolute) conclusion
- 1 pt: Includes several map elements but omits major pieces (e.g. no careful conclusion, or no bias/conflict)
- 0 pts: Accepts one side without credibility analysis, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require every micro-detail from the paragraph if the main credibility tensions appear (double-counting boardings; one rainy morning; unreleased fare-card data).
- Do not require formal labels if the map contents are clearly present.
- Do not require a final expand/cancel decision; careful suspension or “wait for data” is the canonical careful conclusion.
- Quotation optional.

## Question 8: Part 5 Q1 – Review: essay checklist overclaim (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Examples:** four students improved after using the checklist. **Overclaim:** the checklist guarantees better writing for every student. **Revised claim:** the checklist may help some students improve drafts or is worth testing more broadly.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Examples: “four students improved.” Overclaim: “guarantees better writing for every student,” “works for all,” “always improves writing.” Revised: “may help some,” “helped these four,” “worth trying/testing,” “often helps when used carefully.”
- **Required meaning:** All three: examples, overclaim, and a narrowed revised claim. Omitting the revision or the overclaim fails this 1-pt item.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Names examples, overclaim, and a narrowed revised claim
- 0 pts: Missing a required element, keeps the guarantee claim, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal word “overclaim” if “too strong / not proved for everyone” is clear.
- Do not require exact model revision wording.
- Review skill is Week 12 induction sizing, not Week 13 testimony.
