# U1L8LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week8/lit-examples/U1L8LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L8LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L8LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 8 equivocation / ambiguous-middle diagnosis to Macbeth's prophetic language: *fair*, present versus future titles, and the security promises.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Equivocation:** one word used in two senses inside one chain of reasoning.
- **Ambiguous middle:** an apparent linking term that changes idea and therefore does not genuinely connect the claims.
- **Sense A / Sense B:** the two contextually relevant meanings of a contested expression; students need not use these labels unless the prompt does.
- **Fixed-meaning rewrite:** restating the relevant words so one interpretation is explicit and the hidden shift can no longer create a false guarantee.
- **Present fact vs future claim:** what is already true or established versus what is predicted, promised, or not yet established from the hearer's position.
- **Retest:** ask whether the original conclusion still follows after the meanings are made explicit.

Plot summary alone does not earn points.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Two senses of fair (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Two distinct ordinary senses carried by *fair* in Passage 1, such as (1) morally good, right, or just and (2) attractive, pleasant, favorable, promising, or fortunate-sounding.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Also accept “beautiful” versus “good,” “clear/favorable weather” versus “foul/bad conditions,” or another pair grounded in the chant and Banquo's description. The two answers may come from different lines, but they must be genuinely different meanings rather than two near-synonyms for the same sense.
- **Required meaning:** The student must give two contextually defensible senses of *fair*. The prompt does not require a full argument reconstruction or quotation.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives two distinct, passage-grounded ordinary senses of *fair*
- 1 pt: Gives one clear sense, or gives two expressions that are too vague or too similar to establish a real distinction
- 0 pts: Gives meanings unrelated to Passage 1, supplies plot summary only, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact pair “morally good” and “promising.”
- Accept close paraphrase without quotation.
- Do not count “good” and “positive” as two senses unless the student distinguishes different ideas, such as moral goodness versus favorable appearance or prediction.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Fact vs future (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** From Macbeth's point of view in Passage 1, **Thane of Glamis** is already established, **Thane of Cawdor** is not yet established or confirmed to him, and **king hereafter** explicitly concerns the future. The distinction matters because one known title does not prove that an unfamiliar greeting is true or that a future kingship prediction is guaranteed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Glamis is known/present,” “Cawdor is unconfirmed, surprising, or not yet known to Macbeth,” and “king is a prediction/future promise.” For the significance, accept ordinary language explaining that a present fact or one later confirmation cannot automatically prove every further claim.
- **Required meaning:** The response must classify all three greetings from the viewpoint named in the prompt and explain why established fact, unconfirmed claim, and future prediction cannot be treated as equally proved.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies Glamis as established, Cawdor as not yet established to Macbeth, and king hereafter as future, then explains why those different statuses cannot function as equal proof
- 1 pt: Correctly classifies two of the three greetings, or classifies all three but gives little explanation of why the distinction matters
- 0 pts: Treats all three greetings as the same kind of established fact or all as the same kind of future claim; gives plot summary without the logical distinction; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require detailed plot knowledge beyond the supplied reader.
- Grade from Macbeth's explicitly named viewpoint in Passage 1. Even though Cawdor has been granted elsewhere in the play's sequence, it has not yet been established to Macbeth in the supplied passage.
- Do not require the phrase “stable argument.” Accept an explanation such as “one title being true now does not prove that the kingship prediction will come true.”

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Risky inference (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical model:** “Predictions from this source that are confirmed should be trusted as true; the witches' Cawdor greeting is confirmed; therefore their kingship greeting must also be true.” The apparent linking idea is **a confirmed/reliable witches' greeting or prophecy**.
- **Other acceptable models:** A student may reconstruct a different coherent risky chain, such as “Whatever sounds fair or promising is good guidance; the kingship greeting sounds fair; therefore Macbeth should follow it,” or “A title with which Macbeth is hailed is his secured destiny; he is hailed as future king; therefore kingship is guaranteed or action to obtain it is required.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must contain a recognizable premise-to-conclusion chain in an informal middle-term shape and must name the word or idea doing the linking work. The conclusion may be that Macbeth will be king, that kingship is guaranteed, or that he should act—including criminally—to obtain it, if the supplied premises explain that move.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a coherent informal chain with stated support, a risky kingship/action conclusion, and an identifiable linking word or idea repeated across the support
- 1 pt: Identifies a plausible risky conclusion or linking term but does not build a recognizable connecting inference
- 0 pts: Gives plot summary only, lists the greetings without an inference, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat the canonical reconstruction as a model, not the only acceptable answer.
- Do not require formal categorical wording, an A/E/I/O mood, exactly two premises, or the literal word “middle.” “Even if informal” permits ordinary sentences or arrows.
- A linking term may be a phrase or idea—such as “confirmed prophecy,” “fair promise,” “hail/title,” or “destined kingship”—not only one repeated word, but it must actually connect the student's premises.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Why unstable (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The link is unstable because the argument treats unlike ideas as one proof: an attractive or “fair” promise as a true/just command; a greeting or present title as a guaranteed future destiny; partial confirmation as proof that every prophecy is reliable; or promised kingship as proof that crime is necessary. Once those meanings or claim statuses are separated, the witches' words do not establish that Macbeth must become king by committing a crime.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Grade the explanation against the link the student identifies in this response. Accept any passage-grounded term shift or unstable bridge that distinguishes the two meanings/statuses and explains why the shift fails to prove guaranteed kingship or required crime.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify what changes across the apparent link and explain the resulting proof failure. “The prophecy is tricky” or “the witches lie” alone is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly identifies two different meanings or claim statuses being treated as one and explains why they do not prove kingship by crime
- 1 pt: Recognizes that the link shifts or is unreliable but gives only a vague term analysis or weak proof-gap explanation
- 0 pts: Merely retells the prophecy, treats any greeting as proof that crime is required, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact term “ambiguous middle” if the student clearly explains that the apparent linking idea changes.
- Do not require the student to use the same reconstruction as the canonical answer. The key section must stand alone: fair/attractive versus true or right, present title versus future guarantee, partial confirmation versus universal reliability, and promised result versus required criminal means are all defensible targets.
- A moral objection to murder is not by itself a term-shift explanation; the student must explain why the witches' language fails as proof.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – None of woman born (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Sense A, how Macbeth hears it:** no human man or person born from a woman can harm him, so he takes himself to be safe from ordinary human enemies. **Sense B, the undercutting sense:** a person delivered by Caesarean section—Macduff, “from his mother's womb untimely ripped”—is not counted as “born” in the prophecy's narrow ordinary-childbirth wording and can harm Macbeth.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “naturally born” versus “cut/removed from the womb,” “ordinary childbirth” versus “Caesarean birth,” or another accurate formulation of that distinction. Naming Macduff is optional if the second sense clearly identifies the exceptional mode of delivery.
- **Required meaning:** The response must state both the broad reassuring interpretation Macbeth relies on and the narrower childbirth interpretation that leaves an exception and defeats the guarantee.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly gives Macbeth's broad no-human-enemy reading and the Caesarean/not-ordinarily-born exception that undercuts it
- 1 pt: Gives only one clear sense, or notices an exception without explaining the two readings
- 0 pts: Claims no woman can harm Macbeth, gives an unrelated supernatural explanation, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a quotation, Macduff's name, or the exact phrase “untimely ripped.”
- Do not penalize ordinary modern wording such as “born by C-section” if the student understands why that wording falls outside Macbeth's assumed sense.
- The two senses must explain the false security; merely saying the prophecy has a loophole is incomplete.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Birnam wood (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Sense A:** Macbeth hears “Birnam wood shall come” as the literal forest uprooting itself and walking to Dunsinane, which seems impossible. **Sense B:** soldiers can cut or carry branches from Birnam as camouflage, making the wood appear to advance without the rooted forest moving. Macbeth's “That will never be” does **not** follow under both fixed senses because the soldiers-with-branches event is possible.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “literal moving trees” versus “an army carrying branches/wood,” or another accurate description of the literal and represented movement. The retest may be worded “no,” “not under the second sense,” “his guarantee fails,” or “he is safe only under the literal reading.”
- **Required meaning:** The answer must supply both senses and the retest judgment. Two senses without deciding whether the conclusion survives are incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes literal forest movement from soldiers advancing with branches and states that “That will never be” does not hold under both readings
- 1 pt: Gives both senses but omits or obscures the retest, or gives the correct retest with only one sense clearly explained
- 0 pts: Says the guarantee remains secure under both readings, gives an unrelated event, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “camouflage,” “Dunsinane,” or “fixed senses” if the two interpretations and retest are clear.
- Do not require exact plot narration beyond what is needed to state the branch-carrying interpretation.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Repair (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical model using woman-born:** “No person delivered by ordinary childbirth will harm Macbeth. This does not guarantee safety from a person delivered another way or from every other danger.”
- **Canonical model using Birnam wood:** “Macbeth will not be defeated before people or forces carrying wood from Birnam advance toward Dunsinane. That event is possible, so the statement is conditional rather than a guarantee that he is safe.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any rewrite of either one security argument may earn full credit if it makes the operative meaning explicit and removes or rejects the unjustified conclusion of unconditional safety. A student may narrow the conclusion, expose the remaining exception, or state the condition without claiming it is impossible.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must choose one of the two security arguments, fix its crucial meaning, and show through the rewrite that Macbeth's safety is not guaranteed.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Produces a clear fixed-meaning rewrite of either security argument that exposes an exception/condition and no longer guarantees safety
- 1 pt: Identifies the relevant sense but leaves the rewrite vague or still implies near-total safety
- 0 pts: Restates the prophecy unchanged, merely says Macbeth is unsafe without fixing a meaning, or creates a new unrelated guarantee

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat the models as examples, not required wording.
- Do not require rewriting both security arguments; the prompt explicitly asks for one.
- A successful rewrite need not make a new valid argument for Macbeth's death. It must make the meaning stable and block the old false guarantee.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Capstone (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Week 8's tool treats the prophetic lines as claims whose key expressions must be separated and retested, not merely as mysterious magic. *Fair* can shift from attractive-sounding to good/true guidance; present or confirmed titles can be treated as future guarantees; “woman born” and “Birnam wood shall come” can be heard in broad or narrow senses. Once the relevant meanings are fixed, the move from prophetic words to guaranteed kingship, necessary crime, or unconditional safety fails.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Different paragraph organization and ordinary vocabulary are acceptable. A response may choose representative examples rather than list every model, but it must use relevant material from the supplied passages, explain a genuine meaning/status shift, and show how fixing and retesting it changes the proof claim.
- **Required meaning:** The paragraph must move beyond plot or “magic” by applying the Week 8 process: identify unstable language, make the meanings explicit, and reconsider whether the conclusion is proved.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a coherent paragraph applying meaning-fixing and retesting to relevant examples from the passages and explains why the apparent guarantee or requirement fails
- 1 pt: States the general unstable-language idea but gives thin passage evidence, omits the retest/proof consequence, or relies on only a vague “prophecy is tricky” claim
- 0 pts: Gives magic or plot summary only, treats the prophecies as self-proving, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact technical phrases “equivocation,” “ambiguous middle,” “Sense A/Sense B,” or “retest” if the student clearly performs those conceptual moves.
- Do not require every passage, every prophecy, or the model's sentence order. At least one specific unstable expression and its proof consequence must be explained.
- A response that only says “words can have different meanings” is incomplete unless it applies that principle to these Macbeth passages and the failed guarantee/inference.

- Grade each question from its evaluator-local semantic requirements rather than a general synonym instruction.
