**Total Points:** 16

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 9 circular-reasoning / begging-the-question diagnosis to Brutus's private deliberation and public defense in *Julius Caesar*.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Circular reasoning / begging the question:** the reason assumes the conclusion or works only after the disputed verdict has already been granted.
- **Independent support:** a reason or evidence that can be accepted and tested before the verdict is granted.
- **Restatement:** the conclusion repeated in other words and presented as a reason.
- **Loaded alternative:** an either/or frame that builds an unproved danger or verdict into the available choices.
- **Rewrite:** a version that keeps a clear claim while stating what independently testable evidence would be needed.
- **Present vs future danger:** evidence about what Caesar is or has done versus speculation about what he may become.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Also distinguish circularity from ordinary weakness: a reason that can be accepted without the conclusion may still be too weak to prove it, but it is not a mere restatement of the conclusion.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Opening conclusion (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Brutus announces that Caesar must die: “It must be by his death.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “Caesar should/must be killed,” “Brutus decides Caesar's death is necessary,” “the solution is to kill Caesar,” or another clear paraphrase of the death conclusion.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must state the conclusion announced at the opening of Passage 1, not merely Brutus's worry, motive, or later supporting image.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly states that Brutus concludes Caesar must die or be killed
- 1 pt: Says Brutus decides to act against/stop Caesar but leaves death or killing unclear
- 0 pts: Gives only “Brutus is worried,” identifies a reason rather than the conclusion, gives unrelated plot summary, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a quotation or the exact words “It must be by his death.”
- Present-tense and future-tense paraphrases are both acceptable if the must-die conclusion is clear.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Present vs future (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Present evidence weak or missing:** Brutus admits he knows no personal cause against Caesar and has not known Caesar's affections or passions to overrule his reason. **Future danger relied on instead:** being crowned may change Caesar; ambition may climb and turn against others; Caesar may run to dangerous extremes like a serpent once hatched.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** For the present side, accept either admission above or a clear statement that Brutus identifies no current tyranny, abuse, or capital offense. For the future side, accept the crown-may-corrupt prediction, ambition's ladder, possible extremities, serpent's-egg danger, or a faithful paraphrase of feared future tyranny.
- **Required meaning:** The response must give at least one present absence/weakness and at least one future possibility Brutus uses in its place. The student need not list every line.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Accurately identifies one present evidentiary weakness and one future danger on which Brutus relies
- 1 pt: Clearly identifies only the present weakness or only the future danger, or blurs the time distinction
- 0 pts: Treats future tyranny as an already observed present fact, gives plot summary without both sides, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require exact quotation, all model details, or the terms “present evidence” and “future danger.”
- “Caesar has not yet acted like a tyrant, but Brutus fears the crown could make him one” earns full credit.
- Do not require the student to decide here whether the reasoning is circular; the prompt asks for the evidence/status contrast.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Fair opponent test (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Yes.** A fair opponent could accept Brutus's general reasons—for example, that power can corrupt, Caesar may change if crowned, or ambition can become dangerous—without accepting that Caesar must die now. That gap shows the reasons are not simply identical to the death conclusion, but they still do not independently establish that Caesar will become a tyrant or that killing him is necessary and justified.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** A student may answer “not all of them” or “no” if the explanation accurately identifies a specific loaded reason—such as treating Caesar as a dangerous serpent by definition—that already assumes the future-guilt verdict. Full credit depends on applying the fair-opponent test to a stated reason and explaining whether that reason can be granted before the death conclusion.
- **Required meaning:** The response must answer the question and explain the relationship between Brutus's reasons and “Caesar must die.” It must distinguish accepting a possibility or general principle from accepting necessity of death, unless it identifies a reason that embeds that necessity.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a clear yes/no judgment, identifies at least one main reason, and accurately explains whether a fair opponent could grant it without already granting Caesar's death
- 1 pt: Gives the likely judgment or notes that more proof is needed but does not clearly apply the reason-before-conclusion test
- 0 pts: Says the speech fully proves death solely because Brutus is coherent or honorable, gives plot summary only, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “independent-support test.”
- A strong “yes” answer should not be penalized for observing that the argument may be weak or insufficient rather than strictly circular at every step. That is the logical consequence of being able to accept a reason without accepting the conclusion.
- A “no” answer is not automatically correct; it must identify how a specific reason has already loaded Caesar's future guilt or death-worthiness into its wording.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Fashion it thus / serpent’s egg (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** “Fashion it thus” shows Brutus shaping a case that has little present cause into the needed future-danger verdict. The serpent's-egg comparison treats Caesar as the kind of being that will become dangerous once crowned and treats killing him “in the shell” as justified, instead of independently proving that Caesar will become tyrannical or deserves preventive death.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept an explanation focused on either phrase. For “Fashion it thus,” the student may say Brutus constructs or frames the quarrel because Caesar's present conduct supplies no case. For the serpent image, the student may say it assumes future harmful nature, inevitability of danger, or justification for killing before the harm occurs.
- **Required meaning:** The answer must identify a verdict or disputed danger built into Brutus's framing. Merely explaining what a serpent's egg is or saying the image is vivid is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly explains how either “Fashion it thus” or the serpent's-egg comparison builds unproved future danger/death-worthiness into the case
- 1 pt: Notices that Brutus is shaping the case or describes the image but does not clearly identify the assumed verdict
- 0 pts: Gives image or plot summary only, treats the metaphor as independent proof of future tyranny, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- The prompt says “or”; do not require analysis of both phrases.
- Do not require the exact terms “circular,” “loaded comparison,” “capital cause,” or “assumed verdict.”
- An answer that merely says Caesar is like a snake is incomplete unless it explains that the comparison assumes the dangerous nature still needing proof.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Ambition to death (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The move becomes question-begging if *ambitious* is used as a loaded label meaning not merely “seeks advancement” but “will enslave Rome and therefore deserves death.” Then “I killed him because he was ambitious” includes the death-worthy verdict inside the reason instead of independently proving Caesar's ambition, its tyrannical kind, or the need to kill him.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept explanations that Brutus assumes Caesar is dangerously ambitious because he killed him for ambition, treats the accusation of ambition as self-proving, or defines ambition so that death follows automatically. Also accept a logically careful answer that distinguishes defects: if *ambitious* has an ordinary neutral meaning, the move may be an unsupported premise or weak inference rather than circular; it is circular specifically when the label contains the disputed tyranny/death verdict.
- **Required meaning:** The response must explain how lack of independent proof permits the ambition label or its loaded meaning to assume what it is supposed to justify. A bare statement that ambition is bad is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains that unproved or loaded “ambition” embeds Caesar's dangerousness/death-worthiness in the offered reason, or accurately distinguishes that circular use from a merely weak ambition-to-death inference
- 1 pt: Says ambition is unproved or the inference is weak but does not clearly explain how the verdict is assumed
- 0 pts: Treats the word “ambitious” as automatic proof that killing is justified, gives plot summary only, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the terms “begging the question,” “loaded definition,” “restatement,” or “imminent tyranny.”
- Do not penalize a student for noting that an unsupported ambition claim and a circularly loaded ambition label are different defects; that distinction is rigorous and responsive to “can become circular.”
- The student need not prove whether Caesar was ambitious historically; the task is to analyze Brutus's argumentative move in the supplied passage.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Slaves/free men (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The either/or question assumes that if Caesar lives, Romans will die as slaves, while Caesar's death secures freedom. That disputed enslavement danger is presented as one side of the choice rather than independently proved, so anyone resisting Brutus's conclusion is pressured to appear willing to be a slave, base, or un-Roman.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “false/loaded choice,” “he makes the crowd choose Caesar or freedom,” “he assumes Caesar will enslave Rome,” “he shames dissenters as bad Romans,” or comparable language when the answer explains what unproved danger the pressure relies on.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify both the pressure mechanism and the assumed danger: the crowd is forced toward Brutus's conclusion by treating Caesar's continued life as equivalent to enslavement.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains that the slaves/free-men alternative pressures dissent and assumes, without independent proof, that a living Caesar means enslavement
- 1 pt: Identifies emotional/crowd pressure or a false choice but does not clearly state the assumed enslavement danger
- 0 pts: Treats the alternative as proof that Caesar would enslave Rome, gives general plot summary, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the labels “loaded alternative,” “false dilemma,” “question-begging,” or “emotional force.”
- Calling the speech emotional alone is incomplete because the prompt asks how the pressure assumes the danger still needing proof.
- The student need not discuss every later “base,” “bondman,” or “Roman” line; one accurate account of the pressure is enough.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Non-circular rewrite (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical model:** “Caesar's death would be justified only if independent evidence showed that he intended and was able to use the crown to destroy Roman liberty—for example, specific plans, orders, preparations, or abuses that could be checked. General claims that power may corrupt or that Caesar is ambitious are not enough by themselves.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** The clear claim may be conditional (“death would be justified if...”), negative (“death is not yet justified without...”), or narrower (“the evidence supports caution or limits on power, not death”). Required evidence may include specific present acts, credible plans, concrete abuses, corroborated threats to Roman liberty, or another relevant and independently testable basis. Other defensible rewrites are acceptable.
- **Required meaning:** The rewrite must keep a clear conclusion about whether/when Caesar's death is justified and must state what independent evidence would be required. It must not merely rename Caesar ambitious, dangerous, tyrannical, or death-worthy.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a non-circular rewrite with a clear death/justification claim and at least one concrete type of independent evidence that would bear on it
- 1 pt: Gives a sensible narrowed or conditional claim but leaves the required evidence vague, or lists evidence without a clear claim
- 0 pts: Still argues that Caesar must die because he is death-worthy/ambitious/dangerous without independent criteria, gives no rewrite, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat the canonical model as an example, not required wording.
- Do not require a fixed number of evidence items; one sufficiently concrete and relevant category satisfies the prompt.
- A student may conclude that preventive death is not justified by the available evidence. The task assesses non-circular structure, not agreement with Brutus.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Capstone (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Week 9's tool asks whether Brutus's reasons can be accepted and tested before accepting his conclusion. His private speech is coherent in moving from fear of corrupted power to the serpent's-egg image, and his public speech is coherent and public-spirited in opposing slavery to freedom. Yet coherence does not prove that Caesar will become a tyrant, that ambition means enslavement, or that death is necessary. Those claims require independent evidence rather than loaded images, labels, or alternatives.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Different paragraph structures and judgments are acceptable if they use relevant details from the supplied passages, explain how the speech holds together or persuades, and then test whether its key support independently proves the death conclusion. A student may correctly distinguish a merely insufficient inference from a genuinely conclusion-loading reason.
- **Required meaning:** The paragraph must explain the difference between a coherent/persuasive speech and a proved conclusion by applying the Week 9 reason-before-verdict test to Brutus's passages. Plot summary or a bare fallacy label is insufficient.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a coherent paragraph using relevant evidence from both passages to explain why Brutus's speeches can hang together while still lacking independent proof for Caesar's death
- 1 pt: States the coherence-versus-proof distinction but uses only one passage, gives thin textual application, or leaves the independent-support test vague
- 0 pts: Gives plot summary only, treats sincerity/coherence as proof, supplies only a keyword, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact terms “circular reasoning,” “independent support,” “loaded alternative,” or “coherent speech” if the student clearly expresses and applies those concepts.
- Do not require quotation or the model's sentence order.
- An answer should not be denied credit for accurately observing that some of Brutus's premises can be granted without accepting death and are therefore insufficient rather than strictly circular; Week 9's test should preserve that distinction.

## Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:
- Whole points only. Total: 8 × 2 = 16 points.
- Students may identify both circularity and ordinary weakness. Credit accurate distinctions rather than forcing every defect into the circularity label.
