**Total Points:** 16

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 16 rhetoric-vs-proof analysis to Antony’s funeral oration and Lady Macbeth’s persuasion of Macbeth.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Persuasion / rhetorical force:** audience movement and felt power of the speech.
- **Proof:** support that actually establishes the claim under debate.
- **Logos / ethos / pathos:** reason and examples; speaker character/stance; emotion.
- **Claim under proof:** the exact conclusion still needing support.
- **Force matches / exceeds / replaces proof:** judgment about whether persuasion is backed by, outruns, or substitutes for support.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Quotation is optional unless needed for clarity. Credit students who refuse both cynicism (“all rhetoric is fake”) and naivete (“moving speech = proved claim”). Passage-grounded alternatives that preserve force-vs-proof limits earn full credit.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Antony’s claim and desired response (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Antony presses that Caesar was not simply the ambitious tyrant Brutus described; that the killing is grievous/treasonous; and that the crowd should grieve, distrust the conspirators, and turn against them. Desired response: pity for Caesar, anger at the conspirators, and political reversal.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Claims/pressure: Caesar not merely ambitious; conspirators dishonorable; murder is wrong/treason; crowd should mourn and revolt in judgment. Desired response: grief, rage, mutiny of opinion, reject Brutus’s story, political turn. One clear claim-cluster plus one clear desired response is enough.
- **Required meaning:** Main claim pressure **and** wanted audience response. Plot summary alone fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names claim-pressure and audience response
- 1 pt: One element only or thin wording
- 0 pts: Plot summary only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require Antony’s full political program; a coherent claim + response pair is enough.
- Do not require formal “claim under proof” wording.
- Quotation optional.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Ethos and pathos in Antony (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Ethos: “He was my friend, faithful and just to me”; reluctant “not to praise him”; repeated “honourable man” stance that borrows Brutus’s credit while undercutting it. Pathos: poor who cried, shared fall of countrymen, wounds/mantle grief, “bloody treason” language.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Ethos: friendship, loyalty, modest speaker stance, irony with “honourable men,” personal bond with Caesar. Pathos: tears, grief, wounds, mantle, pity, anger language, public mourning cues. One clear ethos and one clear pathos move.
- **Required meaning:** Prompt asks for **one ethos and one pathos** move. “Antony is emotional” without examples fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: One clear ethos and one clear pathos move
- 1 pt: Only one appeal clearly identified
- 0 pts: Says Antony is emotional without examples, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require multiple examples per appeal.
- Accept ordinary “credibility move / emotion move” if correctly matched.
- Do not require logos here (not asked).

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Antony’s logos-style evidence (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Examples such as captives/ransoms filling public coffers and Caesar weeping for the poor pressure the “ambitious” charge. Limited point: those acts do not look like hard, self-seeking ambition of “sterner stuff.” That may weaken Brutus’s label without fully proving Caesar’s whole character or settling whether killing him was justified.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Evidence: ransoms, captives, public benefit, tears for the poor, crown refusals if in the reader materials used. Limited support: challenges “ambitious,” shows public-minded acts, weakens Brutus’s label. Must keep the support **limited**, not total victory.
- **Required meaning:** Logos-style evidence **and** a limited supported point. Treating examples as total victory fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names evidence and a limited supported point
- 1 pt: Names evidence only, or limited point without evidence
- 0 pts: Treats examples as total victory, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both ransoms and tears if one example is well limited.
- Do not require the phrase “sterner stuff.”
- The limited-point job is essential for full credit.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Force vs stronger conclusions (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Antony’s force often **exceeds** or begins to **replace** full proof for stronger conclusions: that the conspirators are simply bloody traitors, that Caesar’s death is wholly unjustified, or that the crowd’s next political act is settled. The speech powerfully moves judgment; it does not complete a full logical case for every conclusion the crowd may adopt.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Exceeds / replaces / outruns proof for treason narrative, full justification judgment, or political next steps. “Matches” only if carefully restricted to a narrow anti-ambition point the student defends with evidence. Must distinguish force from full proof on the **stronger** conclusions the prompt names (treason and what Rome should do).
- **Required meaning:** Force-vs-proof judgment on stronger conclusions **with reason**. “He is persuasive” without claim-level distinction is partial. “Antony proves everything” fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Judges force vs proof on stronger conclusions with reason
- 1 pt: Says he is persuasive without distinguishing claims
- 0 pts: Plot only, “Antony proves everything,” or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Prefer exceeds/replaces for the stronger political conclusions.
- Do not require all three stronger conclusions listed in the model; one well-explained stronger conclusion is enough with a clear force judgment.
- Do not require cynicism about all rhetoric.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Lady Macbeth’s aim and appeals (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** She wants Macbeth to carry out the murder / recover the courage of his earlier hope. Appeals include pathos/shame (green and pale; love accounted little), identity/ethos pressure on manhood and valor matching desire, and practical logos-like reassurance (“we’ll not fail” with a plan).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Aim: do the murder, stop hesitating, be a man, keep his word/hope. Appeals: shame, love-withdrawal threat, manhood taunt, courage identity, plan/assurance, emotional pressure. At least aim + two appeal types, or aim + clear multi-appeal description, matches “which appeals.”
- **Required meaning:** What she wants **and** which appeals she uses. “She is mean” without analysis fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States aim and identifies appeals
- 1 pt: Aim or appeals only
- 0 pts: Calls her mean without analysis, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require formal logos/ethos/pathos labels if the appeal types are clear.
- Do not require every model appeal; two distinct pressures plus aim is strong full credit.
- Quotation optional.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Remaining burden after Lady Macbeth (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Still unproved: that murder is morally permissible; that manhood requires the deed; that success is assured; that Duncan’s trust duties no longer bind; that ambition plus shame equals justification. Emotional settlement is not moral or factual proof.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any clear remaining moral or factual burden: murder still wrong/unjustified; manhood definition not settled as proof; plan may fail; duties remain; desire ≠ justification. Ordinary language is fine.
- **Required meaning:** A real remaining moral/factual burden after emotional success. Saying her speech settles the matter fails. Vague “murder is bad” without tying to unproved burden is weaker (partial).

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names a real remaining moral/factual burden
- 1 pt: Vague “murder is bad” or thin unproved note
- 0 pts: Says her speech settles the matter, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model’s full unproved checklist; one specific remaining burden is enough.
- Do not require students to moralize beyond the logic task.
- Emotional landing ≠ proof is the key distinction.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Rhetoric-vs-proof map (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** A strong map for **either** passage includes claim, audience, key appeals, what is actually supported, what is only suggested, and a force-vs-proof judgment. Example (Antony): claim = Caesar wronged / conspirators dishonorable; audience = Roman crowd; appeals = friendship ethos, grief pathos, anti-ambition examples; proved/limited = some public-benefiting acts; suggested = full treason narrative and required political revenge; force exceeds full proof.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Lady Macbeth map: claim/do the murder; audience = Macbeth; appeals = shame/manhood/plan; limited support = practical plan pieces if any; suggested = murder is manly/necessary/safe; force exceeds moral proof. Other passage-grounded maps that hit the listed jobs.
- **Required meaning:** Prompt allows **one** passage. Jobs: claim, audience, key appeals, proved, only suggested, force vs proof. Plot summary fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Coherent complete map
- 1 pt: Partial map
- 0 pts: Plot summary, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- One complete map is enough; do not require both passages here.
- Do not require the model’s exact line assignments if jobs are accurate.
- Compact form is fine.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Comparison (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Both speeches can be powerful because they recruit identity, emotion, and selective reasons. Power does not settle the claim that needs proof: whether Caesar’s death is justified / what Rome should do, or whether Macbeth should murder Duncan. Rhetoric can prepare attention or distort judgment; logic still asks what follows from what.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any comparison of **both** passages explaining why force can outrun proof while still naming the unsettled claims. Uneven length OK if both appear.
- **Required meaning:** Both passages + force-without-settling-proof structure. Plot only fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Compares both and explains force without proof
- 1 pt: One passage well or thin comparison
- 0 pts: Plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require equal treatment length.
- Do not require formal vocabulary if the force/proof distinction is clear for both.
- Do not accept “all rhetoric is fake” as a substitute for the comparison task.

- Plot summary alone never earns points.
