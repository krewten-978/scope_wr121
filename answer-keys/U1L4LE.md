# U1L4LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week4/lit-examples/U1L4LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L4LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L4LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply the Week 4 Whately habit of mapping practical syllogisms, naming terms, and testing whether the middle term truly connects the argument in Julius Caesar.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Major term:** the predicate of the conclusion; the class or judgment being asserted of the minor term.
- **Minor term:** the subject of the conclusion; the person or case the conclusion is about.
- **Middle term:** the linking term that appears in the premises but not the conclusion. It must connect the minor term to the major term.
- **Pressure-point premise:** the premise on which the argument's success most depends, often because it decides whether the minor term really belongs under the middle term.

Grade for logical mapping and explanation, not for a single mandated wording.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Brutus's conclusion (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical answer: Brutus is trying to justify killing Caesar before Caesar is crowned and can become dangerous—a conclusion he expresses as killing the serpent “in the shell.”

Acceptable alternatives include “Caesar should be killed before he becomes king,” “Caesar should be stopped before gaining supreme power,” or “prevent Caesar from being crowned before he can become a tyrant.” Because the passage’s stated practical act is death, an answer using only “stopped” must still make clear that it means preventing Caesar’s rise in the passage’s preemptive logic, not merely debating or criticizing him.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States a clear practical conclusion that Caesar should be killed or preemptively stopped before coronation/power because of the danger Brutus thinks he may become.
- 1 pt: Identifies prevention of Caesar’s rise or a general anti-Caesar aim but does not state a complete practical conclusion, its preemptive timing, or the proposed action clearly.
- 0 pts: Gives only plot summary, imagery, or a claim Brutus does not advance.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require quotation or the exact phrase “killed in the shell.” Accept concise paraphrase when it preserves Caesar as the target, prevention before full power as the timing, and killing/stopping as the practical judgment.
- Do not accept “Caesar is already a tyrant”; Brutus explicitly reasons from what Caesar may become.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Premises behind Brutus's argument (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical map:
- **Major premise:** Anyone likely to become a dangerous tyrant if given supreme power should be killed or stopped before gaining that power.
- **Minor premise:** Caesar is likely to become a dangerous tyrant if crowned or given supreme power.

Accept differently worded premise pairs when one states a general prevention rule and the other applies the rule’s danger condition to Caesar. For example: “People who would use a crown to do grave harm should be prevented from obtaining it; Caesar would become such a person if crowned.”

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies exactly the requested two components—one possible major premise and one possible minor premise—that share a future-danger category and jointly support Brutus’s preemptive conclusion.
- 1 pt: Supplies only one usable premise, reverses the major/minor labels while still stating both relevant claims, or gives two claims whose logical connection is incomplete but recognizably follows Brutus’s future-danger/prevention reasoning.
- 0 pts: Gives only quotations, themes, or observations; states no premise structure; or treats Caesar’s present tyranny as an established fact contrary to the passage.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model wording, a quotation, or additional evidence. The two required logical jobs are a general rule connecting likely grave future danger to prevention and a Caesar-specific application of that condition.
- Do not accept two Caesar-specific observations with no general prevention rule, or two general claims with no application to Caesar, for full credit.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Brutus's terms (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Minor term:** Caesar.
- **Major term:** a person who should be killed/stopped before gaining supreme power, or the judgment “should be killed/stopped before gaining power.”
- **Middle term:** a person likely to become a dangerous tyrant or grave danger if given supreme power.

The required meaning is that Caesar is the subject of the conclusion, preemptive killing/stopping is what the conclusion predicates of him, and likely future tyranny/danger is the bridge used in the premises but not asserted as the conclusion itself.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Correctly supplies all three requested roles in ordinary language, even if the phrases differ from the model.
- 1 pt: Correctly supplies two roles, or supplies all three with one role too vague to distinguish it from another.
- 0 pts: Correctly supplies at most one role, merely writes S/P/M, or confuses the proposed action with the future-danger bridge throughout.

**Notes for grading:**
- Accept “Caesar”; “those who should be prevented from taking power”; and “likely future tyrants” as a complete set. Also accept “dangerous ruler if crowned,” “likely to abuse supreme power,” or “a grave future threat under a crown” for the middle term when it performs the same linking job.
- Do not require the exact words “minor,” “major,” and “middle” inside the term phrases if the response clearly labels the three requested roles. Do not accept “power” or “ambition” alone as a complete middle term; either word alone does not state the danger category linking Caesar to the proposed prevention.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Pressure-point premise (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical answer: The **minor premise**—the Caesar-specific claim that Caesar will probably become a dangerous tyrant if crowned—is the pressure point. Brutus would need evidence that Caesar himself is likely to abuse supreme power or become gravely dangerous, such as relevant evidence from Caesar’s character, actions, intentions, or likely conduct. The mere general possibility that power can corrupt does not establish the claim about Caesar.

Accept “the premise about Caesar” or “the claim that Caesar will become dangerous” instead of the formal label “minor premise” when its role is unambiguous. The prompt asks which of the two premise roles is the pressure point, so a response that calls it the major premise does not correctly answer that component.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies the minor/Caesar-specific premise and explains that Brutus must prove Caesar is genuinely likely to become a dangerous tyrant or abuse crowned power.
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies the minor/Caesar-specific premise but gives only a vague explanation such as “he needs more evidence,” or explains the needed Caesar-specific proof while omitting the premise label/identification.
- 0 pts: Chooses only the major premise, says the serpent image or mere possibility proves Caesar’s danger, or supplies neither the premise identification nor the needed proof.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a particular kind or number of evidence examples. One accurate statement of what must be established about Caesar is enough.
- Do not require the phrase “middle-term application”; accept ordinary language that shows the same issue—that Caesar must actually fit the likely-dangerous-future-ruler category.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Cassius's conclusion (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Representative canonical conclusion: Rome and Brutus should resist Caesar’s one-man dominance; Caesar should not be allowed to stand above Rome and its other nobles.

Other defensible conclusions include “Brutus should join action against Caesar,” “Romans should stop accepting themselves as Caesar’s underlings,” or “Caesar should not rule Rome alone.” An alternative must be a practical or political judgment Cassius is trying to move Brutus toward, not merely an image or a statement that Caesar is powerful.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States one clear, passage-grounded conclusion about what Brutus/Romans should do or how Caesar should not stand in relation to Rome.
- 1 pt: Gives a recognizable anti-Caesar political aim but leaves the action or judgment vague.
- 0 pts: Summarizes the Colossus image, reports only that Caesar is great, or gives an unrelated conclusion.

**Notes for grading:**
- The prompt explicitly permits one possible conclusion, so do not require all model formulations or a single literary interpretation. Accept an unlisted conclusion when it fits Cassius’s effort to move Brutus from Roman shame/inequality toward resistance to Caesar’s dominance.
- Quotation is optional, not required.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Cassius's middle term (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Possible middle terms include:
- a man falsely or dangerously treated as greater than Rome’s other nobles;
- a ruler whose one-man dominance makes Romans underlings;
- a threat to Roman liberty;
- a man whose elevation dishonors or subordinates free Romans.

Evaluation: Cassius suggests such categories through the Colossus image, Caesar’s greatness, Roman shame, Brutus’s equal name, and the language of “underlings,” but he does not fully prove that Caesar actually belongs in a category that warrants resistance. His rhetoric and honor-based comparisons do not by themselves establish dangerous domination or a threat to liberty.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names one plausible linking category in Cassius’s reasoning and explains whether—and how well—the passage establishes that Caesar fits it. Full credit may conclude “not proved,” “only partly supported,” or offer a defensible qualified judgment grounded in the passage.
- 1 pt: Names a plausible middle term but gives no meaningful evaluation, or evaluates Cassius’s support without clearly identifying the linking category.
- 0 pts: Gives only a major/minor term, repeats “Caesar” or “Rome” without a linking category, or offers an evaluation unrelated to whether Caesar fits the proposed middle term.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require one listed middle term. An unlisted term is acceptable when it connects evidence about Caesar’s position to a resistance/anti-dominance conclusion and the student tests whether Caesar has been shown to fit that category.
- A bare label such as “bad ruler,” “power,” or “honor” is too vague for full credit unless the explanation develops it into a clear linking category.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Comparing discipline (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical judgment: Brutus’s serpent’s-egg argument is more logically disciplined because its premise structure, conclusion, and middle term can be mapped more clearly. Cassius’s Colossus argument is rhetorically forceful but mixes claims about honor, equality, resentment, greatness, and liberty, making the connecting premise and middle term less precise. Brutus’s clearer structure does not make his minor premise true or his action morally right.

Because the prompt asks “which,” a student may defend Cassius instead, but only with a specific, passage-grounded comparison showing why Cassius has the clearer premises, conclusion, and middle term. Mere disagreement with Brutus or preference for Cassius is not an adequate alternative.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Chooses one argument and compares both arguments using all three explicitly requested technical terms—premise, conclusion, and middle term—with accurate reasoning supporting the choice.
- 1 pt: Makes a reasonable comparative judgment but omits one or more of the required terms, discusses only one argument, or gives only limited explanation of the logical structure.
- 0 pts: Chooses based only on character preference, morality, or rhetorical appeal; gives no comparison; or uses the required words without an accurate logical explanation.

**Notes for grading:**
- The worksheet explicitly requires the terms “premise,” “conclusion,” and “middle term”; do not give full credit when any is absent merely because the general preference is correct.
- Do not require students to say Brutus is factually sound or morally right. “More disciplined” concerns clarity of logical mapping, not truth or morality.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Whately's middle-term question (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
Canonical middle term: a person who is genuinely likely to become a grave future danger or tyrant if allowed to gain power, where that danger is serious and probable enough to make violent prevention justified.

Accept alternatives such as “a proven likely future tyrant,” “someone whose rise creates an imminent grave threat that can only be prevented beforehand,” or “a person likely to abuse power so severely that preemptive violence is warranted.” The term must do both logical jobs: identify more than a mere possible danger and connect that danger to the strong conclusion of violent prevention.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States a middle category that requires sufficiently probable, grave future danger and makes that danger relevant to the proposed violent prevention.
- 1 pt: Identifies future danger or likely tyranny but leaves either probability/gravity or the connection to justified violent prevention unclear.
- 0 pts: Offers only possibility, fear, dislike, ambition, vivid imagery, or political disagreement; treats any possible future danger as enough; or gives no linking category.

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “possibility is not proof” or a separate warning sentence; the prompt asks for the middle term. Full credit is earned when the proposed term itself clearly exceeds mere possibility and links to the strength of the conclusion.
- Accept responsible generalizations beyond Julius Caesar. Do not accept a term that would justify violence against anyone who might possibly cause harm.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. No half points.
- Total: 8 questions × 2 points = 16 points.
