# U1L15LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 16

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week15/lit-examples/U1L15LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L15LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L15LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply Week 15 verbal / real / mixed dispute mapping to Macbeth and Lady Macbeth's dispute over manhood and Brutus / Antony's public dispute over Caesar's ambition and honor.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Verbal dispute:** a disagreement mainly over how a word is being used.
- **Real dispute:** a disagreement that remains after the word is clarified.
- **Mixed dispute:** a disagreement containing both contested word meaning and a real disagreement underneath.
- **Contested term:** the powerful word whose meaning is disputed or pressured.
- **Shared claim:** something both sides could accept once terms are clarified.
- **Remaining disagreement:** what still needs evidence, proof, or judgment after clarification.
- **Dispute map:** contested term; each side's meaning; shared claim; remaining disagreement; classification.

Plot summary alone does not earn points. Quotation is optional unless the student needs it to make the analysis clear. Grade dispute jobs, not one exclusive literary reading, when several passage-grounded readings preserve the logical task.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Lady Macbeth's use of man (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Contested term: **man** (manhood / masculinity / being a man). Lady Macbeth’s meaning: bold, daring, willing to carry out the murder / courage proved by doing the violent deed he promised.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Term: “man,” “manhood,” “what it means to be a man,” “masculinity.” Her meaning: bravery as violence, prove you’re a man by killing, courage = following through on the murder, taunt that hesitation is unmanly. Ordinary paraphrases grounded in the passage are fine.
- **Required meaning:** Identify the contested term and explain Lady Macbeth’s pressuring sense. Plot summary without the term/meaning fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies “man” (or clear equivalent) and explains Lady Macbeth's meaning accurately
- 1 pt: Identifies the term but gives a thin meaning
- 0 pts: Plot summary only, incorrect term, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require every model adjective (bold, daring, murder); one clear pressuring sense is enough.
- Do not require the formal label “contested term” if the word and her meaning are clear.
- Quotation optional.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Macbeth's meaning of man (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Macbeth’s reply suggests manhood includes **moral limits**: he dares what “may become a man,” but doing more makes someone less than truly human or morally monstrous. Courage is tied to fitting action, not mere violence.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Moral boundary, fitting/human action, courage with limits, not-monster standard, manhood ≠ any daring act, refuse deeds that go beyond what becomes a man. Paraphrases of the “may become a man” idea are fine without exact quotation.
- **Required meaning:** Contrast with Lady Macbeth: Macbeth’s sense includes a moral or human limit, not only boldness. Treating Macbeth as simply cowardly without analyzing the term fails full credit.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains Macbeth's contrasting meaning with moral boundary/fitting action
- 1 pt: Says Macbeth disagrees but gives little definition
- 0 pts: Treats Macbeth as simply cowardly without analyzing the term, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrase “may become a man.”
- Do not require calling him “right” or “wrong.”
- A clear limit-vs-violence contrast earns full credit.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Remaining Macbeth disagreement (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** After clarifying the word, they still disagree about the **deed itself**: whether murdering Duncan is courage or moral excess; whether ambition justifies action; whether loyalty/hospitality should restrain Macbeth; whether doing the deed would make him more admirable or less human.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any one clear remaining real fight: kill or not; morality of the murder; ambition vs duty; whether the deed is manly or monstrous; loyalty to Duncan. Compact answers are fine.
- **Required meaning:** A **real** disagreement that survives word-clarification. Saying the disagreement disappears once “man” is defined fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names a real disagreement that remains after word clarification
- 1 pt: Gives a vague disagreement about murder or courage
- 0 pts: Says the disagreement disappears once “man” is defined, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model’s full checklist; one specific remaining real issue is enough.
- Do not require formal “real dispute” label if the remaining fight is clearly non-verbal.
- Do not require settling who is right.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Macbeth dispute classification (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Classification: **mixed**. Verbal part: “man” carries different meanings. Real part: even after defining manhood, they still disagree whether murder is courageous, fitting, loyal, or morally corrupt.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** “Mixed” with both parts explained. Carefully justified “mostly verbal with a real moral dispute underneath” can earn full credit if both layers appear. Pure verbal only or pure real only without addressing the other layer is weaker.
- **Required meaning:** Prompt asks to classify as verbal, real, or mixed **and** explain. Full credit needs a classification **and** reasons covering word-fight and remaining deed/moral fight (for mixed).

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Classifies as mixed (or carefully equivalent) and explains both verbal and real parts
- 1 pt: Gives a plausible classification with weak explanation, or only one layer
- 0 pts: Classifies without reasons, summarizes plot, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Prefer mixed; do not award full credit for “verbal” alone if the student ignores the remaining murder disagreement, or “real” alone if the student ignores the word pressure.
- Do not require identical model wording.
- Explanation matters as much as the label.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Brutus's claim and contested term (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Brutus claims Caesar was **ambitious** and that he killed Caesar because of that ambition (for Rome’s good). Contested term the crowd must understand: **ambitious** (broader speeches may also pressure “honourable,” but ambition is the primary claim-term here).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Claim: Caesar was ambitious; Brutus killed him for ambition/liberty; Caesar threatened Rome by ambition. Term: “ambitious,” “ambition.” Mentions of “honourable” as a second pressured word are acceptable extras, not required.
- **Required meaning:** Prompt asks for Brutus’s claim **and** the contested term. Both required for full credit.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States Brutus's claim and identifies the contested term
- 1 pt: Gives claim or term only
- 0 pts: Incorrect, plot summary only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require “honourable” unless the student uses it accurately as additional pressure.
- Do not require full speech summary.
- Ordinary language is fine.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Antony's examples and pressure (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Antony points to Caesar bringing captives/ransom money to Rome and weeping for the poor. These pressure Brutus’s “ambitious” because they suggest public benefit and compassion rather than hard self-seeking ambition (“sterner stuff”).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Examples: ransoms/public coffers, captives, tears for the poor, refusals of the crown if present in the reader materials the student uses, mantle/wounds as related pathos-evidence if clearly tied to challenging the ambition charge. Pressure: doesn’t look like selfish ambition; public-minded; soft-hearted; undermines Brutus’s label.
- **Required meaning:** At least one concrete Antony example **and** how it challenges the contested term/ambition charge. “Antony is sarcastic/persuasive” alone fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names Antony's examples and explains how they challenge the term
- 1 pt: Names examples but gives little connection to ambition, or connection without examples
- 0 pts: Says only that Antony is sarcastic or persuasive, plot only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both ransoms and tears if one example is well connected to ambition.
- Do not require the phrase “sterner stuff.”
- Quotation optional.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Caesar dispute map (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Contested term: ambitious. Brutus’s meaning: dangerously power-seeking / threat to Roman freedom. Antony’s pressure: ransom money and tears for the poor do not fit that harsh meaning. Shared claim: if Caesar were dangerously ambitious that would be serious; Caesar was powerful/politically important. Remaining disagreement: whether Caesar’s actions prove dangerous ambition and whether killing him was justified. Classification: **mixed** — word meaning matters, but evidence and political judgment remain disputed.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Ordinary-language map covering the prompt’s jobs: contested term; Brutus’s meaning; Antony’s pressure; shared claim; remaining disagreement; classification. Shared claim may be thinner (e.g. ambition would matter if true; Caesar held power). Remaining disagreement may focus on ambition evidence or on justification of the killing. Classification mixed preferred.
- **Required meaning:** Prompt lists six map jobs. Grade contents of those jobs. Plot summary without dispute analysis fails.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Completes a coherent dispute map with classification
- 1 pt: Includes some map elements but incomplete
- 0 pts: Plot summary without dispute analysis, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model’s full shared-claim or remaining-disagreement inventory; each job present in substance is enough.
- Do not require quotation.
- Compact bullets or paragraph form is fine.

## Question 8: Part 4 Q2 – Why clarification helps but does not settle (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** In both passages, clarifying the powerful word helps reveal the real issue (manhood is not just daring; ambition is not just holding power). Clarification does **not** settle whether Macbeth should kill Duncan or whether Caesar was dangerous enough to kill. Real conflict still needs moral judgment, evidence, and proof.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any comparison that (a) treats both Macbeth and Caesar/Antony–Brutus material and (b) explains help-from-clarification plus remaining unsettled real dispute. Uneven length is fine if both passages appear.
- **Required meaning:** **Both** passages for full credit, plus the help-without-settling structure. “Definitions solve everything” or single-passage-only answers fail full credit.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Compares both passages and explains clarification plus remaining dispute
- 1 pt: Discusses one passage well or gives a thin comparison
- 0 pts: Says definitions solve everything, plot summary only, or no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require equal paragraph length for each passage.
- Do not require the model’s exact moral inventory.
- Passage-grounded alternatives that keep help-plus-remaining-real-fight earn full credit.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**
- Whole points only. Total: 8 × 2 = 16 points.
- Credit students who see mixed disputes: Shakespeare's speakers fight over words, but the words carry real moral and political disagreements.
- Plot summary alone never earns points.
