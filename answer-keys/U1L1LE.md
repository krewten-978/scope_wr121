# U1L1LE – Lit Example Worksheet Answer Key & Rubric

**Total Points:** 12

**Paired reader:** `units/elements-of-logic-week1/lit-examples/U1L1LE_lit_example_reader.tex` / `.pdf`.

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L1LE/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L1LE**).

**Purpose:** Students apply the Week 1 Whately four-step method to two Macbeth passages. Grade for logical structure and textual grounding, not for plot summary. Accept varied phrasing when the logical roles are clear.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

Use these terms consistently when evaluating student answers:

- **Conclusion:** the claim the speaker is trying to prove, justify, or move toward.
- **Stated reason:** the evidence, fact, observation, or support the speaker actually gives.
- **Hidden assumption:** the unstated idea that must be true for the stated reason to prove the conclusion.
- **Follow-test:** the student’s judgment about whether the conclusion really follows from the stated reason once the hidden assumption is made visible. A follow-test may say **yes**, **no**, or **only partly**, but it must explain the logical connection rather than merely state an opinion.

Do not require students to use these exact labels if their logical roles are clear. Do require the grader to interpret “follow-test” in this precise sense throughout the rubric.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Macbeth’s first inference from prophecy (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical conclusion:** Macbeth is tempted to conclude that the remaining royal prediction has real force and that he may become king.
- **Canonical stated reason:** The witches’ Cawdor prediction has been confirmed: Macbeth has been named Thane of Cawdor.
- **Canonical hidden assumption:** Because one prediction came true, the witches’ remaining prediction is reliable, destined, or likely to come true as well.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** The conclusion may be framed as “chance may make Macbeth king,” “the prophecy is trustworthy,” “the kingship prediction may be coming true,” or “Cawdor is a sign/first step toward kingship.” The stated reason may be paraphrased as the confirmation of Macbeth’s new title. The hidden assumption may be any explicit bridge from one fulfilled prediction to confidence in the larger unfulfilled prediction.
- **Required meaning:** The response must separate the confirmed fact about Cawdor from a larger conclusion about the prophecy or kingship and must state the unstated bridge between them.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Clearly identifies a textually defensible tempted conclusion, the confirmed Cawdor prediction as the stated reason, and a hidden assumption that links partial confirmation to the larger conclusion
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies two of the three requested elements, or identifies all three but leaves the hidden link vague
- 0 pts: Gives only plot summary or moral judgment, does not identify the confirmed Cawdor news as support, or supplies one or fewer requested elements

**Notes for grading:**  
- Do not require one exact conclusion; the prompt explicitly allows “one conclusion Macbeth is tempted to draw.”
- Do not require a follow-test in this question. Although the worksheet’s general purpose mentions the four-step method, this question specifically asks only for conclusion, stated reason, and hidden assumption.
- Do not accept “Macbeth must murder Duncan” as a conclusion proved by the Cawdor news. The witches do not command murder, and confirmation of one title does not establish that murder is necessary or justified.
- Brief paraphrase is sufficient; quotation is optional.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Banquo’s warning about truthful predictions (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical conclusion:** Macbeth should be cautious about trusting or following the witches’ predictions.
- **Canonical stated reason:** Deceptive or evil powers may tell small truths (“honest trifles”) in order to win trust and lead people into greater harm.
- **Canonical hidden assumption:** A source that tells some truths may still use them deceptively, so partial truth does not guarantee trustworthy guidance or harmless consequences.
- **Canonical follow-test:** Yes, or at least strongly enough to justify caution. Banquo does not prove that every prediction is false; he gives a reason not to infer total trustworthiness from a true detail.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept conclusions such as “Macbeth should not blindly trust the witches,” “truthful predictions can be bait,” or “one true prediction does not make the witches reliable.” Accept “largely yes,” “partly,” or “the warning follows as a precaution” when the explanation preserves the limited conclusion of caution rather than claiming that Banquo disproves all prophecy.
- **Required meaning:** All four requested logical elements must be present, and the answer must distinguish a statement’s truth from the speaker’s trustworthiness or purpose.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Reconstructs all four elements and explains why small truths can support caution without proving that every witch’s statement is false
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies Banquo’s cautious conclusion and the “truths used to harm” reason but omits or blurs the hidden assumption or follow-test
- 0 pts: Says only that Banquo is afraid or suspicious, gives plot summary without argument structure, or claims that telling one truth proves the witches wholly trustworthy

**Notes for grading:**  
- Do not require the phrases “honest trifles,” “instruments of darkness,” or “trustworthy guide”; accurate paraphrase is sufficient.
- Do not require a blanket judgment that the witches are lying. Banquo’s supported conclusion is caution about how truths may be used.
- A bare “yes,” “no,” or “partly” does not satisfy the follow-test for full credit because the prompt asks students to reconstruct the warning as an argument.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Macbeth’s argument against murdering Duncan (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical conclusion:** Macbeth should not murder Duncan; the murder would be wrong, dangerous, or self-defeating.
- **Canonical stated reasons:** The student must choose **two** reasons Macbeth gives. Accept any two of the following: violence taught by the murderer returns against him; justice gives the murderer his own poisoned cup; Duncan is Macbeth’s kinsman; Macbeth is Duncan’s subject; Macbeth is Duncan’s host and should protect rather than kill him; Duncan has ruled virtuously/meekly; Duncan’s virtues will testify against the murder; or the murder’s consequences cannot simply be contained.
- **Canonical hidden assumption:** The selected reasons count against murder—for example, one ought not violate duties of kinship, loyalty, and hospitality; murder that invites justice and destructive consequences should be rejected; or a virtuous ruler should not be killed.
- **Canonical follow-test:** Yes, the selected reasons give strong support for not murdering Duncan, especially when taken together.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** The conclusion may be moral (“killing Duncan would be wrong”), prudential (“Macbeth should not act because the violence will return against him”), or a combination. The hidden assumption need not mention every listed duty; it must connect the student’s two chosen reasons to that version of the conclusion. Accept a qualified “yes/strongly supports” rather than requiring a claim of deductive certainty.
- **Required meaning:** The response must include the conclusion, exactly or at least two distinct passage-based reasons, a bridge explaining why those reasons count against murder, and an evaluation of whether the reasons support the conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Supplies all four requested components, including two distinct and accurate textual reasons, with a coherent hidden assumption and follow-test
- 1 pt: Gives a correct conclusion and only one accurate reason; or gives two accurate reasons but omits or substantially weakens the hidden assumption or follow-test
- 0 pts: Gives no accurate passage-based reason, merely summarizes the scene, or treats ambition as one of Macbeth’s reasons proving that murder should occur

**Notes for grading:**  
- Do not require direct quotation or the model’s sentence order. Brief textual reference or accurate paraphrase is sufficient.
- “Macbeth is Duncan’s kinsman and subject” may count as two reasons if the student clearly treats kinship and political loyalty as distinct obligations; “Duncan trusts Macbeth as kinsman, subject, and host” may also summarize multiple duties accurately.
- Do not require one universal hidden assumption. It must fit the two reasons the student actually chose.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Motive is not proof (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical distinction:** A motive explains what pushes a person to want or do something; proof supplies support showing that a claim is true or that an action is justified, necessary, or rightly chosen.
- **Canonical application:** Macbeth’s “vaulting ambition” explains his desire or temptation to murder Duncan, but it does not show that Duncan deserves death, that murder is right or necessary, that Macbeth must act, or that prophecy excuses him. Naming ambition may instead expose that desire—not evidence—is driving him.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “cause/drive/reason why he wants it” for motive and “evidence/logical justification/reason showing he should do it” for proof. Accept concise applications such as “ambition explains Macbeth’s choice but does not justify it” or “wanting the crown is not evidence that killing Duncan is right.”
- **Required meaning:** The answer must both distinguish the two concepts and apply the distinction to Macbeth. It must preserve the difference between psychological explanation and logical or moral support.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives an accurate motive-versus-proof distinction and a specific application showing that ambition may explain Macbeth’s temptation but does not establish or justify the murder
- 1 pt: Correctly states either the distinction or the Macbeth application but leaves the other vague, incomplete, or merely implied
- 0 pts: Treats ambition as proof that Macbeth should kill Duncan, gives only plot/theme summary, or never distinguishes explanation of desire from support for a conclusion

**Notes for grading:**  
- Do not require the exact words “psychological,” “logical,” “motive,” or “proof” if the contrast is unambiguous.
- A response need not make the additional claim that ambition weakens Macbeth’s case; that is an acceptable insight, not a required element.
- Because the prompt asks for an explanation and an application, a keyword-only answer such as “motive ≠ proof” is incomplete.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Testing a too-quick inference (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical models:** Defensible examples include moving from “the Cawdor prediction came true” to “Macbeth’s kingship is guaranteed”; from “the witches predicted kingship” to “Macbeth is not responsible for how it happens”; from Macbeth’s imagining murder to “murder is necessary”; or from the possibility of containing consequences to “the murder will have no consequences.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any specific moment from either supplied passage may earn credit if the student identifies (1) the larger possible conclusion, (2) the actual textual support, (3) the hidden assumption connecting them, and (4) whether the conclusion follows. The moment need not match a listed model. Conclusions, support, and assumptions may be paraphrased, but their logical roles and strength must remain distinct.
- **Required meaning:** The chosen conclusion must be a genuine overreach from a possibility, fear, desire, or prediction in the passages. The follow-test should normally be “no,” “not yet,” or “only partly,” with an explanation that the support establishes at most a possibility, temptation, or limited fact rather than the larger claim.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Selects a specific text-based overreach and supplies all four requested elements with a defensible follow judgment
- 1 pt: Selects a relevant moment and correctly supplies two or three elements, or names all four but leaves the hidden assumption or follow explanation too vague to establish the gap
- 0 pts: Gives only a general theme or plot statement, does not identify actual support from either passage, or endorses a materially stronger conclusion than the evidence supports without recognizing the overreach

**Notes for grading:**  
- Do not require one predetermined scene, quotation, conclusion, or hidden assumption; the prompt explicitly permits choice.
- Accept a concise answer and any sentence order if all four logical roles are unambiguous.
- Do not accept unrelated outside plot events as the actual support; the student must choose a moment from one of the two supplied passages.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – What follows from what? (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**  
- **Canonical supported-claim models:** The witches correctly predicted Macbeth’s new title; Macbeth has reasons to fear moral or practical consequences from murdering Duncan; Macbeth is driven by ambition; or Banquo has reason to urge caution.
- **Canonical unsupported/not-yet-proved models:** Macbeth must become king by murdering Duncan; prophecy removes Macbeth’s responsibility; ambition proves murder is justified; one fulfilled prediction settles the future; or success can contain all consequences of murder.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any pair of claims grounded in the supplied passages may earn credit if one is reasonably supported and the other is genuinely unsupported or stronger than the evidence. The student may use a claim not listed above and may paraphrase the text.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify one claim in each category and logically contrast their support—for example, evidence may establish a present fact, motive, warning, or reason for caution without establishing future necessity, moral permission, or certainty.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies one reasonably supported claim and one unsupported/not-yet-proved claim and explains the evidential difference between them
- 1 pt: Gives two plausible claims but weakly explains which is supported and why, or clearly analyzes only one side of the contrast
- 0 pts: Gives only one claim, reverses the supported and unsupported categories without a defensible explanation, or offers plot summary/opinion with no comparison of support

**Notes for grading:**  
- Full credit requires a contrast, not merely two separate observations.
- Do not require the four formal labels from earlier questions; this prompt asks for two claims and an explanation of the difference.
- Do not require extensive quotation. Brief textual grounding or accurate passage-specific paraphrase is enough.
- A supported claim need not be certain beyond all doubt; “reasonably supported” means the cited passage gives relevant evidence of the appropriate strength.

**Overall Notes for Grading This Worksheet:**  
- Whole points only. No half points.  
- Grade the logic of the student’s analysis, not polished prose or exact model wording.
- Do not require extensive Shakespeare quotation. Brief textual grounding is enough when the argument structure is clear.  
- Total: 6 questions × 2 points = 12 points.
