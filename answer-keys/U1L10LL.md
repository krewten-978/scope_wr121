## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Point at issue:** the exact question the argument must answer for its conclusion to be proved.
- **Irrelevant proof / missing the point:** evidence may prove something, but not the conclusion that needed proof.
- **Nearby claim:** a related claim that may be true or supported while the larger conclusion remains unproved.
- **Partial proof:** support that proves only part of what the argument needs.
- **Repair:** adding evidence that answers the real issue or narrowing the conclusion to fit the evidence.
- **Loaded definition / circular reasoning (review):** a definition or reason that builds the disputed verdict into a key term or restates the conclusion.
- **Only-translation (review):** “Only F do X” / “Only F are X” standardly means all that do X / all X are F (not the reverse “all F do X”).

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Senior trip bus drivers (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The point at issue is whether the senior trip should be moved from Friday to Monday. Friendly bus drivers may support a nearby claim that the bus company is pleasant or easy to work with, but that does not answer whether the trip date should change. Relevant proof would concern scheduling, conflicts, safety, cost, availability, weather, or other date-related reasons.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “should we move the trip to Monday,” “whether Friday or Monday is the right date,” or a clear paraphrase of the date-change question. For the miss, accept ordinary language such as “friendly drivers don’t settle the day,” “that answers how nice the company is, not when to go,” or “wrong question.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must state the date-change issue and explain that friendly drivers answer a different question. Naming “irrelevant” alone without the issue is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: States the point at issue (trip date / Friday-to-Monday move) and explains why friendly drivers miss that issue
- 1 pt: Correctly says the evidence is irrelevant or misses the point but does not clearly state the issue, or states the issue but leaves the miss vague
- 0 pts: Treats friendly drivers as sufficient for the date change, invents an unrelated issue, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact phrases “point at issue,” “nearby claim,” or “irrelevant proof.”
- Do not require a list of multiple relevant-evidence types; one clear explanation of the mismatch is enough.
- Do not require the student to rewrite the argument.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Easy article (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The evidence proves, at most, that the article is short, readable, or accessible. What remains unproved is whether it is reliable enough to cite in a research paper. Reliability would need something like source expertise, evidence quality, citations, accuracy, publication context, corroboration, or freedom from serious bias or distortion.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “it proves the article is easy to read,” “readability/accessibility only,” or “style/length, not trustworthiness.” For the unproved side, accept “reliability,” “accuracy,” “whether we can trust it,” “whether it is good research support,” or a concrete missing check such as “author credentials,” “sources cited,” or “corroboration.”
- **Required meaning:** The response must distinguish what short/easy reading actually supports from the reliability claim still needing proof. Listing every reliability criterion is not required.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes readability/accessibility from research reliability and names what remains unproved or what kind of proof is missing
- 1 pt: Makes a partial distinction (e.g., says “not enough” without saying what the evidence does prove, or names reliability without saying easy reading is only nearby)
- 0 pts: Says easy reading proves reliability, collapses both claims, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require all model reliability criteria. One accurate missing-proof category is enough.
- Do not require formal terms “nearby claim” or “partial proof.”
- Accept concise answers such as “Proves easy to read; does not prove trustworthy for research.”

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Riverfront plan (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Nearby claim that may be proved: the current riverfront looks ugly / needs improvement. Larger conclusion still needing proof: the city should choose this particular riverfront plan. Ugliness may support a need for some change without settling cost, alternatives, usefulness, environmental effects, or priority for this plan.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “the waterfront is ugly,” “it needs improvement,” or “people agree it looks bad” for the nearby claim. For the larger conclusion, accept “this plan should be chosen,” “the city should pick that proposal,” or “we should adopt the plan.” Students may mention missing plan-specific evidence.
- **Required meaning:** Both sides requested by the prompt must appear: a nearby claim that ugliness may support, and the larger plan-choice conclusion that remains unproved.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies the nearby ugliness/improvement claim and the unproved larger conclusion that this plan should be chosen
- 1 pt: Accurately names only one of the two sides, or blurs them together
- 0 pts: Treats ugliness as full proof for choosing the plan, reverses the two claims, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require every missing evidence type (cost, environment, alternatives). Naming the two claims is the core task.
- Do not require the labels “nearby claim” and “larger conclusion” if the two contents are clearly distinguished.
- Agreement that the riverfront is ugly may be treated as true or merely “may be proved”; either framing is fine.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Team captain points (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Scoring the most points may prove a nearby claim that Jordan is a strong scorer or strong player. It does not by itself settle whether Jordan should be team captain, because captaincy may also require leadership, communication, judgment, reliability, sportsmanship, or team trust.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “proves Jordan scores well / is a good offensive player / had a strong season statistically” for the nearby claim. For the miss, accept “captain is more than points,” “doesn’t prove leadership,” or naming one missing captaincy criterion.
- **Required meaning:** The response must separate a scoring/player claim the evidence may support from the captaincy decision that still needs more (or different) proof.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Explains the nearby scoring/player claim and why captaincy remains unproved
- 1 pt: Partial: notes missing leadership or says “not enough” without stating what points do prove, or states only one side clearly
- 0 pts: Treats most points as automatically sufficient for captain, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a fixed list of captaincy criteria; one relevant missing criterion plus the nearby claim is enough.
- Do not require the phrase “point at issue.”
- A student may note that scoring could be one relevant factor among others; full credit still requires that points alone do not settle captaincy.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Homework policy repair (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical model with added evidence:** “The homework policy is fair because the workload is reasonable for the course level, it is applied the same way to every student, and students can ask questions about assignments before they are due.”
- **Canonical model with narrowed conclusion:** “The homework policy was clearly announced on the first day, so students knew the expectations in advance” (not “therefore the policy is fair”).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Many repairs work. Added evidence may include equal application, reasonable load, accommodations, chance to clarify, consistent enforcement, or another fairness-bearing fact. Narrowing may stop at clear announcement, advance notice, or transparency without claiming full fairness. Wording need not match the models.
- **Required meaning:** The rewrite must either give independent fairness-relevant support or narrow the conclusion so it matches what clear announcement can support. It must not keep “fair because it was announced” as the whole case.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Complete non-mismatched repair: clear conclusion plus either concrete fairness-relevant evidence or a genuinely narrowed claim that fits announcement
- 1 pt: Improves the argument but leaves support vague, leaves the conclusion unclear, or still half-relies on announcement as fairness
- 0 pts: Repeats “fair because announced/clear,” supplies no repair, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Treat models as examples, not required content. One sufficiently concrete fairness reason satisfies the add-evidence path.
- Do not require both adding evidence and narrowing.
- A different judgment about homework policy is acceptable if the logical repair matches evidence to claim.

## Question 6: Part 4 Q1 – Roof and scoreboard paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Main conclusion (coach):** the scoreboard purchase should come first / before the roof. **Evidence offered:** sports unify the school, the basketball team made the playoffs, students cheer louder when the gym looks modern. **Nearby point that may be proved:** sports or new scoreboards can have morale, unity, or appearance value. **Question still unanswered:** whether that value should outrank replacing the leaking library roof (safety, water damage, cost, library needs, priority).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept paraphrases such as “buy scoreboards first,” “scoreboards before the roof,” or “prioritize the gym upgrade.” Evidence may be summarized rather than quoted line by line. Nearby point may be “sports matter,” “morale is real,” or “the gym would look better.” Unanswered issue may focus on roof safety/damage, budget priority, or whether sports success settles facilities ranking. Students may briefly note the principal’s roof evidence as the competing issue.
- **Required meaning:** All four components the prompt requests must appear: main conclusion, evidence actually offered, nearby point proved, and unanswered question. The coach’s priority conclusion is the main target of the paragraph task.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Accurately supplies all four required components
- 1 pt: Accurately supplies two or three of the four, or supplies all four with one materially vague or swapped
- 0 pts: Supplies one or none correctly, treats the principal’s roof argument as the only conclusion without the coach’s priority claim, treats sports success as settling priority, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require every evidence detail or both the principal’s and coach’s full cases. The four requested components are enough.
- Do not require formal labels if the four contents are clearly present.
- Identifying the principal’s roof-first claim as context is fine; full credit still needs the coach’s scoreboard-first conclusion as the paragraph’s closing claim under analysis.

## Question 7: Part 5 Q1 – Review: fair rules (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Under the standard reading of **only**, “Only fair rules let everyone speak before a vote” means that all rules that let everyone speak before a vote are fair rules (not the reverse “all fair rules let everyone speak”). Structure: All rules that allow full pre-vote speaking are fair; this rule allows that speaking; therefore this rule is fair. **The conclusion follows** (valid categorical form).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept ordinary-language equivalents such as “if a rule lets everyone speak, it counts as fair on that standard,” “nothing but fair rules allow that speaking,” or mood/form labels that preserve the same direction (e.g., AAA with middle = rules that let everyone speak). Accept “valid,” “follows,” or “yes, the conclusion follows” when paired with a structure that correctly handles **only**.
- **Required meaning:** The response must identify the structure/form being used and judge whether the conclusion follows. Full credit requires a directionally correct handling of **only** (or an equivalent exclusive reading) and a follows judgment that matches that structure.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Gives a structure consistent with the standard only-translation and correctly says the conclusion follows; or states the exclusive reading in plain language and says it follows
- 0 pts: Reverses **only** into “all fair rules let everyone speak” and calls the argument invalid on that mistranslation; says valid/invalid with no usable structure; treats the item as circular without addressing form; or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a specific medieval mood name or the word “Barbara.”
- Do not require the exact phrasing “all rules that let everyone speak are fair” if the exclusive force of **only** is clear and the follows judgment matches.
- **Content note:** An earlier key version reversed **only** and treated the item as an invalid converse. Grade the printed wording, not that older model. Students who reverse **only** and diagnose invalidity are not demonstrating the form correctly on this prompt.
- If a student explicitly flags two readings of the English and shows which reading yields follows vs. not, credit the accurate exclusive reading as the standard logical answer.

## Question 8: Part 5 Q2 – Review: true leader (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Main defect: loaded definition / circular reasoning. “True leader” is defined so that changing one’s mind automatically means Maya is not a true leader; the verdict is built into the definition rather than independently proved by leadership-relevant evidence.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “loaded definition,” “circular,” “begging the question,” “the definition assumes the conclusion,” “true leader is defined to exclude changing your mind,” or “restates the verdict inside the term.” Ordinary language is enough if it locates the built-in disqualification.
- **Required meaning:** The response must name a prior-week defect and briefly explain that the definition (not independent leadership evidence) produces the not-a-true-leader conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Names loaded definition, circularity, or begging the question (or a clear ordinary-language equivalent) and briefly explains how the definition builds in the verdict
- 0 pts: Calls the form invalid without addressing the definition, says only that Maya should change her mind, accepts the definition as proof, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both “loaded definition” and “circular reasoning” if one is clearly explained.
- Do not require the student to rewrite the argument or supply a better definition of leadership.
- A response that only says “unfair” without pointing to the definition is incomplete.
