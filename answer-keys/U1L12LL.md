**Total Points:** 16

**Paired worksheet:** `graded-assignments/U1L12LL/worksheet.tex` / `.pdf` (Assignment ID **U1L12LL**).

**Paired reading:** Week 12 — induction, examples, general claims, sample, representative sample, cherry-picking, counterexample, overclaim, evidence ladder.

## Grading Vocabulary for This Key

- **Induction:** reasoning from observed cases toward a broader claim.
- **Example / observation:** a particular case offered as evidence.
- **General claim:** a conclusion about a larger group, pattern, or rule.
- **Sample:** the set of examples actually examined.
- **Representative sample:** examples that fairly reflect the larger group being judged.
- **Counterexample:** a case that challenges or disproves an overlarge claim.
- **Overclaim:** a conclusion broader than the evidence supports.
- **Revised claim:** a narrower conclusion that fits the evidence.
- **Evidence ladder:** observations → pattern → limited claim → overclaim → revised conclusion.
- **Review terms:** prior-week skills only: analogy source/target/relevant similarity and irrelevant proof / missing the point.

## Question 1: Part 1 Q1 – Flash cards (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Example:** one student used flash cards before one vocabulary quiz and scored higher than usual. **General claim:** flash cards are the best study method for everyone. **Size of claim:** very broad / universal or near-universal (all students, all situations, “best for everyone”).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** For the example, accept “one student / one quiz / higher score with flash cards.” For the general claim, accept “flash cards are best for everyone,” “everyone should use flash cards,” or “flash cards are the best study method.” For size, accept “universal,” “very broad,” “too large for one case,” “applies to everyone,” or “overgeneralized.” Students may also note that one case is too thin for that conclusion; that is helpful but not a separate required fourth element.
- **Required meaning:** All three prompt elements must appear: the observed case, the broad general claim, and a clear sense that the claim’s scope is large/universal relative to the evidence. Treating the one quiz as the general claim is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Identifies example, general claim, and broad/universal size (in ordinary language)
- 1 pt: Identifies two of the three elements accurately, or all three with one materially vague
- 0 pts: Treats one quiz as enough proof for everyone with no separation of claim size, invents unrelated content, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the formal words “induction,” “sample,” or “overclaim” on this item.
- Do not require exact worksheet wording; clear paraphrase is enough.
- Do not require a full revised claim here; Part 3 covers revision.
- Accept “the claim is too big / too strong for one example” as covering size when paired with identification of example and claim.

## Question 2: Part 1 Q2 – Club reminders (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Examples:** three meetings in one month had higher attendance when same-day (morning-of) reminders were sent. **Limited claim:** same-day reminders probably help attendance (for this club / are worth continuing or testing).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “three meetings with higher attendance after morning reminders” for examples. For the limited claim, accept “reminders probably help,” “same-day reminders may improve attendance,” “worth continuing reminders,” or another cautious claim close to the observed club pattern. Students may note “probably,” small sample, or one club as reasons the claim is limited.
- **Required meaning:** Both the observed cases and a limited (not universal) claim must appear. The claim must stay near the evidence, not jump to “all clubs forever” or “reminders always work everywhere.”

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names the examples and the limited claim accurately
- 1 pt: Names only examples or only the limited claim, or confuses limited vs overbroad claim while partly right
- 0 pts: Misses the evidence-to-claim relation, treats the line as a universal proof, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the word “limited claim” if the student states a cautious claim that matches the evidence.
- Do not require listing every meeting detail; “three meetings / higher attendance with morning reminders” is enough.
- Do not require a counterexample or revision on this item.

## Question 3: Part 2 Q1 – Dessert-line survey (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** The sample shows what dessert-line customers (or bakery-interested people already waiting for dessert) may want. It does **not** fairly show whether the whole town needs more bakeries. A larger town-wide conclusion would need evidence from a broader mix of residents and relevant factors (demand, costs, existing options, non-dessert customers, etc.).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “shows dessert customers / people who already want dessert / bakery fans in line” for what the sample shows. Accept “does not prove the whole town needs more bakeries,” “biased sample,” “unrepresentative of the town,” or “cherry-picked audience” for the larger-conclusion problem. For better evidence, accept any clear broader mix: other residents, people not in the dessert line, businesses, costs, existing bakeries, different times/locations.
- **Required meaning:** Must distinguish (1) what this biased/narrow sample can support from (2) the larger town-need claim that needs better evidence. Saying only “bad survey” without that contrast is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Distinguishes what the biased sample shows from the larger claim needing better evidence
- 1 pt: Notes bias or unrepresentativeness with only a thin explanation of either side
- 0 pts: Treats the dessert line as representative of the whole town, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require a fixed checklist of missing evidence types; one or two clear better-evidence ideas are enough when the sample/claim contrast is clear.
- Do not require the terms “sample,” “representative,” or “cherry-picking” if the bias and scope problem are clear in ordinary language.
- Do not require a full research design.

## Question 4: Part 2 Q2 – Parking complaints (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Five senior complaints may show that some seniors dislike the parking rules. Missing or unrepresentative evidence includes views from other grades, students without cars, staff, safety/cost information, and whether the five complainers represent most seniors. Concluding that the entire student body wants immediate change is an **overclaim**.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept any specific missing groups or factors: juniors/sophomores/freshmen, non-drivers, bus riders, staff, parents, safety data, majority vs vocal minority, opposite opinions from seniors who like the rules. Accept “overclaim,” “too broad,” “not enough for the whole student body,” or “five seniors ≠ entire student body.”
- **Required meaning:** Must explain why five senior complaints do not represent the entire student body (missing groups and/or unrepresentative sample) and reject or limit the whole-body immediate-change conclusion.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Names missing or unrepresentative evidence and identifies the whole-body conclusion as too strong / an overclaim
- 1 pt: Says only “needs more evidence” without specifics, or names missing groups without linking to the overclaim
- 0 pts: Accepts five complaints as enough for the whole student body, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require every model missing-evidence item; one or two well-chosen missing groups/factors plus the overclaim point is enough.
- Do not require the formal word “overclaim” if “too strong / not supported for everyone” is clear.
- Do not require students to invent a new parking policy.

## Question 5: Part 3 Q1 – Group projects (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Counterexample:** a group project where work was divided fairly and members completed assigned roles (or another real case where not one person did all the work / the project did not fail). **Revised claim:** e.g. “Some group projects fail when roles and accountability are unclear,” or “Group projects often need clear structure to prevent unequal work.”
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Any plausible counterexample that falsifies “every group project fails because one person always does all the work” (successful equal-share project; project that failed for a different reason; one-person load without total failure). Any useful narrowed revision that keeps a real caution without “every / always”: “some,” “often when…,” “can fail if…,” “without clear roles….”
- **Required meaning:** Both a counterexample and a revised narrower claim are required. The revision must remain useful (still warn about unequal work or structure) and must not restating the universal claim.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Gives a plausible counterexample and a useful narrowed revision
- 1 pt: Gives only counterexample or only revision, or both with one off-target
- 0 pts: Repeats the universal claim, offers no usable counterexample/revision pair, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the model’s exact revision wording.
- Do not require students to claim group projects never fail.
- A counterexample that only says “my project was fine” without enough detail can still earn credit if it clearly challenges the universal claim; prefer cases that touch division of labor or success.

## Question 6: Part 3 Q2 – Online tutorials (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** Two weak tutorials can support a **limited** claim such as “these tutorials did not teach this difficult skill well” or “some online tutorials are poor / did not work for this skill.” They **cannot** support “no online tutorial can teach a difficult skill,” which is universal and would be challenged by any strong tutorial or learner success.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Supported: “these two were weak,” “some tutorials fail,” “online tutorials are not always enough,” “these didn’t teach the hard skill.” Unsupported: the printed universal “no online tutorial can…,” “all online tutorials fail,” “impossible to learn a hard skill online.” Students may note that one good tutorial would counterexample the universal claim.
- **Required meaning:** Must separate what two weak cases support from the universal negative they do not prove. Treating two failures as proof against all tutorials is incorrect.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Separates a supported limited claim from the unsupported universal claim
- 1 pt: States only one side clearly, or both sides with one vague
- 0 pts: Treats two tutorials as proof against all tutorials, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the words “universal” or “overclaim” if the can-support / cannot-support contrast is clear.
- Do not require inventing a specific successful tutorial title; noting that success cases would refute the universal is enough when present.
- Do not require a full revised school policy on online learning.

## Question 7: Part 4 Q1 – Writing center paragraph (2 points)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer (evidence ladder):**
  - **Observations:** four Thursday after-school sessions; on three Thursdays more students came after 3:30 than at lunch; after-school students mostly wanted long-essay help while lunch students wanted quick citation help.
  - **Pattern:** after-school may be better attended or better suited for long essay help (relative to lunch on those days).
  - **Limited claim:** keep, expand, or test some after-school writing-center hours for essay help.
  - **Overclaim:** lunch tutoring is useless and all hours should move after school.
  - **Revised conclusion:** shift some hours after school, keep some lunch hours for quick help, or test a mixed schedule before eliminating lunch.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept paraphrases of the same five ladder steps. Observations may be summarized as “four Thursdays; three with higher after-school attendance; different help needs” without listing every clause. Pattern may be “after school draws more / longer-help students.” Limited claim may be “after-school hours are valuable for essays.” Overclaim must capture the coordinator’s extreme move-all-hours / lunch-is-useless conclusion. Revision must narrow away from eliminating all lunch hours without testing alternatives.
- **Required meaning:** The prompt asks for all five ladder elements: observations, pattern, limited claim, overclaim, and revised conclusion. Omitting the coordinator’s overclaim or offering no revision is incomplete.

**Rubric:**
- 2 pts: Includes observations, pattern, limited claim, overclaim, and revised conclusion (ordinary language OK)
- 1 pt: Includes most elements but omits one major step, or all five with one materially wrong
- 0 pts: Accepts the all-hours conclusion without qualification, confuses the ladder entirely, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the exact labels “observations / pattern / limited claim / overclaim / revised conclusion” if the five contents are clearly present in order or clearly labeled in other words.
- Do not require quoting the paragraph; accurate paraphrase is enough (“Quote briefly when helpful” is not on this prompt—paraphrase is fine).
- Do not require a perfect five-line template; a compact paragraph that hits all five elements earns full credit.
- Partial credit is appropriate when the student clearly rejects the overclaim and revises but under-specifies observations/pattern, or maps observations well but keeps the overclaim.

## Question 8: Part 5 Q1 – Review: classroom boat analogy (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Source case:** steering a boat. **Target case:** running a classroom. **Relevant similarity:** one responsible leader must keep order/direction when conditions change (so clearer directions during transitions can be supported if kept narrow).
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Source: “boat / steering / captain.” Target: “classroom / teacher / teaching.” Relevant similarity: “need one person to keep order,” “direction when conditions change,” “leadership through transitions,” “someone steers when things shift.” Students may add that the analogy supports clearer transition directions if kept narrow; that is helpful but the prompt only requires source, target, and relevant similarity.
- **Required meaning:** All three: source, target, and a likeness that matters for the clearer-directions conclusion. Swapping source and target is a material error.

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Correctly identifies source, target, and relevant similarity
- 0 pts: Swaps cases, misses a required element, gives only decorative likeness with no leadership/order link, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require the labels “source” and “target” if the two cases are clearly distinguished in the right roles.
- Do not require a full disanalogy or overextension analysis; this review item only asks for source, target, and relevant similarity.
- Ordinary paraphrases are enough.

## Question 9: Part 5 Q2 – Review: energetic speech (1 point)

**Expected Answer:**
- **Canonical answer:** **Earlier-week defect:** irrelevant proof / missing the point. Energy and humor may show the candidate is lively or engaging, but they do not prove the candidate has the best transportation plan.
- **Acceptable alternatives:** Accept “missing the point,” “irrelevant proof,” “wrong issue,” “proves a different claim,” “doesn’t address transportation,” or “evidence doesn’t match the conclusion.” Explanation must show the mismatch between proved trait (energy/humor) and claimed conclusion (best transportation plan).
- **Required meaning:** Name the defect type (in course or clear ordinary language) and briefly explain the proof-to-claim mismatch. A label alone with no mismatch explanation is incomplete for full credit on a 1-pt item that asks to “name … and explain.”

**Rubric:**
- 1 pt: Names missing the point / irrelevant proof (or clear equivalent) and explains the mismatch
- 0 pts: Wrong defect, label only with no explanation, treats energy as proof of the transportation plan, or gives no relevant answer

**Notes for grading:**
- Do not require both formal phrases “irrelevant proof” and “missing the point”; one accurate name plus explanation is enough.
- Do not require Week-number citation or the exact course slogan if the mismatch is clear.
- Do not accept “ad hominem,” “circular,” or “false premise” as the primary diagnosis unless the student also clearly describes the irrelevant-proof mismatch (those labels are off-target for this item).
